# Motion, time and depth cues that resolve overlap ambiguity at junctions (crossing vs branching, depth order) — primate evidence and implementable mechanisms for vessel-flow video

_Source note: author lists, titles, volumes, pages and DOIs were checked against PubMed (NCBI E-utilities, via the PubMed connector) and, for papers PubMed does not index (Kaplan 1969, Gibson et al. 1969, Metzger 1934, Layton & Yazdanbakhsh 2015, Barnes & Mingolla 2013, Hu et al. 2018), against Crossref. Unless stated otherwise, the quantitative values below come from the published abstracts as PubMed returns them. Full texts were not read. Every "Inference" bullet applies the findings to a fixed-weight network running on stabilized microscopy video of red blood cells (RBCs) flowing in vessels, some in focus and some defocused. These bullets are reasoning, not established findings. "[Assumption]" marks claims about the imaging domain that the vision literature here does not source._

## 1. Motion transparency: how MT/V5 represents two overlapping motions, psychophysical limits on segregating them, and decomposition models

### Takeaway
V1 direction-selective cells signal each component of two overlapping motions. MT responses are suppressed by opponent-direction inhibition, and that inhibition operates mainly between signals of similar disparity and similar spatial frequency. Transparency is perceived only when the opposing local motion signals are spatially *unbalanced*. The brain appears to read the *shape* of the MT population response rather than look for two separate peaks. Psychophysically, two directions are represented independently once they differ by more than about 20°. Direction alone supports at most two transparent surfaces (three if speed also differs). Segmenting by speed alone needs large speed differences and about 200 ms.

### Cited Findings
**Neurophysiology (macaque V1/MT)**
- **Paradigm:** V1 and MT recordings in behaving macaques, using random-dot fields that formed single surfaces, transparent surfaces or motion discontinuities. **Result:** V1 cells kept responding to their preferred direction under transparency, while MT cells were *suppressed*. The authors concluded that separate V1 subpopulations can represent more than one motion vector at a single retinal location, and that MT's inhibitory direction interactions smooth or average the velocity field. — Snowden RJ, Treue S, Erickson RG, Andersen RA (1991). The response of area MT and V1 neurons to transparent motion. *J Neurosci* 11(9):2768–2785. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.11-09-02768.1991](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.11-09-02768.1991)
- **Paradigm:** "paired" dots (each dot moving in one direction is locally paired with an opposite-moving partner, so the display is locally balanced and looks like flicker) versus "unpaired" dots (looks like two transparent surfaces), recorded in V1 and MT. **Result:** on average, V1 responses could not reliably distinguish the two displays. A large fraction of MT cells, and the MT population average, responded significantly more to unpaired (transparent) dots. MT opponent suppression correlated strongly and negatively with responses to flicker noise. The authors propose a two-stage model: local motion measurement (mainly V1), then local suppression between different directions (mainly MT), whose role is noise reduction. — Qian N, Andersen RA (1994). Transparent motion perception as detection of unbalanced motion signals. II. Physiology. *J Neurosci* 14(12):7367–7380. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.14-12-07367.1994](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.14-12-07367.1994)
- **Paradigm:** human psychophysics with paired and unpaired dots. **Result:** displays balanced in every local region look non-transparent, and every transparent display contained locally unbalanced signals. Balanced displays became transparent again when the two components were placed at **different stereo depths or given different spatial-frequency content**. The authors propose a disparity- and spatial-frequency-*specific* local suppression stage. — Qian N, Andersen RA, Adelson EH (1994). Transparent motion perception as detection of unbalanced motion signals. I. Psychophysics. *J Neurosci* 14(12):7357–7366. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.14-12-07357.1994](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.14-12-07357.1994)
- **Result (macaque MT):** inhibition between motion directions within an MT receptive field occurs **mainly between signals with similar disparities**. Transparent surfaces at different depths are therefore represented without inhibiting each other, while spurious motion signals from a single surface cancel out. — Bradley DC, Qian N, Andersen RA (1995). Integration of motion and stereopsis in middle temporal cortical area of macaques. *Nature* 373(6515):609–611. [doi:10.1038/373609a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/373609a0)
- **Result (macaque MT plus human psychophysics):** perceptual segregation of transparent dot patterns **did not depend on two peaks** in the MT population activity. The overall shape of the population response appeared to determine how many components were seen and in which directions. This produces "direction metamers": patterns containing three or five directions are perceived as only two. — Treue S, Hol K, Rauber HJ (2000). Seeing multiple directions of motion—physiology and psychophysics. *Nat Neurosci* 3(3):270–276. [doi:10.1038/72985](https://doi.org/10.1038/72985)
- **Result (macaque MT):** averaged over the population, the response to two overlapping directions follows the average of the component responses. Within that average, subgroups of neurons can represent component directions separated by **less than the unidirectional tuning width**. Some weight one side's component more heavily; others pool nonlinearly and show two peaks even when the averaged response is unimodal. Direction tuning starts at the **vector average** and evolves toward the **component directions over about 70–100 ms**. — Xiao J, Huang X (2015). Distributed and dynamic neural encoding of multiple motion directions of transparently moving stimuli in cortical area MT. *J Neurosci* 35(49):16180–16198. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2175-15.2015](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2175-15.2015)
- **Result (human and macaque behaviour, macaque MT):** two transparent dot patterns moving in opposite directions look **faster** than a single pattern at the same physical speed, and speed discrimination is worse. MT responses to bidirectional motion are often reduced at the preferred speed, and their speed tuning curves **shift to lower preferred speeds**. A labeled-line decoder applied to these changes reproduces the perceptual speed changes. — Krekelberg B, van Wezel RJA (2013). Neural mechanisms of speed perception: transparent motion. *J Neurophysiol* 110(9):2007–2018. [doi:10.1152/jn.00333.2013](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00333.2013)

**Psychophysical limits on segregating transparent motions**
- **Direction difference:** transparent directions are represented independently at separations **above about 20°** (tested range 5–180°). Judging the two directions jointly is **2–3 times less precise** than judging a single stream. Adding a second stream degrades precision for the first by only **about 1.5 times**. Raising dot speed from 1 to 10 deg/s improved direction precision but did not affect transparency, and dot density between 0.1 and 19 dots/deg² had no effect. — Braddick OJ, Wishart KA, Curran W (2002). Directional performance in motion transparency. *Vision Res* 42(10):1237–1248. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(02)00018-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(02)00018-4)
- **Number of directions:** observers perceive at most **two** directions at once when transparency is defined by direction alone. Bidirectional transparency needs about **42% signal intensity, roughly three times the level needed to detect unidirectional motion**, which implicates V5/MT. — Edwards M, Greenwood JA (2005). The perception of motion transparency: a signal-to-noise limit. *Vision Res* 45(14):1877–1884. [doi:10.1016/j.visres.2005.01.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2005.01.026)
- **Adding speed differences** splits the signals between slow and fast speed-tuned global-motion systems and raises the limit to **three** directions. Contrast-polarity differences give a modest improvement. — Greenwood JA, Edwards M (2006). An extension of the transparent-motion detection limit using speed-tuned global-motion systems. *Vision Res* 46(8–9):1440–1449. [doi:10.1016/j.visres.2005.07.020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2005.07.020)
- **Speed difference alone (same direction, 7°×7° fields):** the speed difference needed to see two surfaces rises **sharply above about 8 deg/s**, and falls with stimulus duration out to **about 200 ms**. Speed *discrimination* thresholds stay low up to at least 16 deg/s, so segmentation and discrimination are dissociable. — Masson GS, Mestre DR, Stone LS (1999). Speed tuning of motion segmentation and discrimination. *Vision Res* 39(26):4297–4308. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(99)00143-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(99)00143-1)
- **Spatial scale of speed segmentation:** perceptual and segmentation performance change over at a corrugation bar width of **about 0.4°**. Speed-segmentation thresholds rise sharply when dots of different speeds are paired within **about 0.2° in the fovea**, a critical distance that grows linearly with eccentricity and stays close to V1 receptive-field size. A stochastic model with V1-sized local pooling fits the data. — Mestre DR, Masson GS, Stone LS (2001). Spatial scale of motion segmentation from speed cues. *Vision Res* 41(21):2697–2713. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(01)00162-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(01)00162-6)
- **Locally paired dots are not seen as null motion:** with components 60–120° apart, observers see coherent motion in the **vector-average direction**, and judge speed by the vector average as well. Abolishing transparency does not abolish a global motion percept. — Curran W, Braddick OJ (2000). Speed and direction of locally-paired dot patterns. *Vision Res* 40(16):2115–2124. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(00)00084-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(00)00084-5)
- **Direction repulsion:** each of two intermingled dot sets can appear displaced by **up to about 20°** away from its companion's direction. — Marshak W, Sekuler R (1979). Mutual repulsion between moving visual targets. *Science* 205(4413):1399–1401. [doi:10.1126/science.472756](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.472756)
- Repulsion occurs when the two directions are **within about 60°** of each other and is minimal at cardinal directions. **Perceiving two directions always produced two planes segregated in depth.** Stereo did not change the size of the repulsion but did **disambiguate which plane was in front**. — Hiris E, Blake R (1996). Direction repulsion in motion transparency. *Vis Neurosci* 13(1):187–197. [doi:10.1017/S0952523800007227](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0952523800007227)
- Total errors in transparent-direction judgments can reach **about 22°**, but the true two-direction interaction contributes **at most about 7°**. The rest is "reference repulsion" that also affects a single motion. — Rauber HJ, Treue S (1999). Revisiting motion repulsion: evidence for a general phenomenon? *Vision Res* 39(19):3187–3196. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(99)00025-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(99)00025-5)

**Junction-specific rule: intersection luminance decides "one pattern" versus "two overlapping layers"**
- Two superimposed moving gratings usually cohere into a single "pattern" motion. Whether they do depends strongly on the **luminance of the intersections relative to the gratings**, in a way that "closely parallels the physics of transparency". When intersection luminance is consistent with one grating lying transparently over the other, pattern motion is replaced by **two independently moving transparent gratings**. — Stoner GR, Albright TD, Ramachandran VS (1990). Transparency and coherence in human motion perception. *Nature* 344(6262):153–155. [doi:10.1038/344153a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/344153a0)
- **Macaque visual cortex (MT):** direction tuning follows this percept. Sensitivity to the *component* gratings rises when the intersections favour non-coherent (transparent) motion. — Stoner GR, Albright TD (1992). Neural correlates of perceptual motion coherence. *Nature* 358(6385):412–414. [doi:10.1038/358412a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/358412a0)
- The original plaid-coherence paradigm (coherent pattern motion versus components sliding over each other): Adelson EH, Movshon JA (1982). Phenomenal coherence of moving visual patterns. *Nature* 300(5892):523–525. [doi:10.1038/300523a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/300523a0) (no abstract in PubMed).

**Computational decomposition models (implementable)**
- **Selection model of MT:** a subset of units with MT-like non-classical receptive fields picks out the image regions where velocity estimates are *most reliable*. The result is a distributed segmentation into disjoint patches that share a common velocity. The model handles occluded and transparent stimuli (including transparent plaids), which spatial-continuity models get wrong. — Nowlan SJ, Sejnowski TJ (1995). A selection model for motion processing in area MT of primates. *J Neurosci* 15(2):1195–1214. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.15-02-01195.1995](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.15-02-01195.1995)
- **Two-stage transparency model:** direction units with inhibitory interactions (which cause repulsion) feed units that pool *direction × disparity*. — Hiris & Blake 1996 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0952523800007227)). Qian et al. 1994 add the disparity- and spatial-frequency-specific suppression stage ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.14-12-07357.1994)).
- **Population-shape decoding:** decide how many directions are present, and which, from the shape of the whole population response rather than by detecting peaks. — Treue et al. 2000 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/72985)).

### Inferences
- **Crossing signature:** where two vessels cross and both flows are visible, the overlap region contains **two velocity vectors at the same pixels** (transparent motion). At a bifurcation, each pixel carries one velocity and the direction changes across space. A local "transparency detector" is therefore a direct cue for crossing versus branching. It could count the direction channels still active after opponent suppression, or measure how bimodal or broad the local direction population is (Treue-style). (Inference)
- **RBC streams are naturally "unpaired":** discrete cells sit at independent positions, so per Qian et al. they should produce locally unbalanced signals and visible transparency. The risk case is dense RBC columns crossing at a small angle. There, local signals may approach balance, and per Curran & Braddick the result is a **vector-average artifact**: a spurious single flow at the mean direction, which could make a crossing look like one vessel. (Inference)
- **Make opponency defocus/SF-specific:** psychophysics shows that different spatial-frequency content rescues transparency, and MT inhibition is disparity-specific. Opponent suppression should therefore act only *within* a spatial-frequency band, or within a defocus-level channel. A sharp vessel (rich in high SF) crossing a blurred vessel (low SF only) then keeps both flows. This maps directly onto a fixed-weight architecture: separate SF bands, with suppression within bands and none across them. (Inference; motivated by Qian et al. 1994 and Bradley et al. 1995)
- **Crossing angle matters:** below about 20° direction separation, two flows may not be represented independently, and true repulsion biases the estimated angle by up to about 7°. Nearly parallel overlapping vessels are the hardest case. They then differ only in speed, which needs large differences and about 200 ms of integration, and becomes unreliable above about 8 deg/s (these are human values and would need rescaling to pixels per frame). (Inference)
- **Temporal integration:** component-direction coding takes about 70–100 ms to emerge in MT, and speed segmentation improves out to about 200 ms. That suggests integrating over at least about 200 ms of video (about 6 frames at 30 fps; frame rate is an assumption). (Inference)
- **Do not trust speed measured inside the overlap:** transparent bidirectional motion biases MT speed coding and perceived speed. Speeds used to link arms of a junction should be measured on the arms, away from the overlap patch. (Inference)
- **Stoner-type X-junction luminance test:** [Assumption] in trans-illumination, RBC-filled vessels absorb light, so where two vessels overlap the transmittance should be roughly the product of the two (Beer–Lambert behaviour, not sourced here). A darker "double-density" overlap patch would then be the image analogue of a transparency-consistent intersection, implying two layers (a crossing). A bifurcation should have no such patch. This parallels the "physics of transparency" rule that human motion integration follows (Stoner et al. 1990). (Inference)

### Gaps
- Snowden et al. (1991) and Treue et al. (2000) abstracts give no numbers (suppression index, or the direction separation at which MT population responses become bimodal). The full texts were not retrieved.
- No studies were found on motion transparency for **streams of discrete particles confined to narrow tubes**, or for one sharp and one defocused component (SF difference is the closest proxy).
- How the multiplicative-versus-additive transparency rules (Stoner et al.) apply to absorptive biological overlaps was not tested in any source found.

## 2. Motion energy and direction selectivity: Adelson–Bergen, Simoncelli–Heeger, speed and direction computation, and robustness to blur and contrast

### Takeaway
The standard implementable pipeline has two stages. First, linear spatiotemporally oriented filters in quadrature pairs are squared, summed (motion energy) and opponently combined. Second, V1-like units are pooled into MT-like velocity-tuned units with rectification and divisive normalization. MT speed tuning is fairly invariant to spatial frequency when a stimulus contains many SFs moving together, which favours robustness to the high-SF loss caused by defocus. Perceived speed is biased, though: lower contrast and higher SF both look slower, and MT preferred speeds shift lower at low contrast. A blurred, lower-contrast vessel's flow would therefore tend to be under-estimated by brain-like decoders.

### Cited Findings
- **Motion energy model:** motion is orientation in x–y–t. The first stage uses linear filters oriented in space-time and tuned in SF. Outputs of quadrature pairs are **squared and summed** to give motion energy, which then feeds an **opponent stage**. The model is consistent with known physiology and psychophysics. — Adelson EH, Bergen JR (1985). Spatiotemporal energy models for the perception of motion. *J Opt Soc Am A* 2(2):284–299. [doi:10.1364/JOSAA.2.000284](https://doi.org/10.1364/josaa.2.000284)
- **Energy filters to optical flow:** a set of spatiotemporal motion-energy filters is combined to extract image flow. Velocity is encoded as the peak of a distribution of velocity-tuned, MT-like units. The model handles the aperture problem and simulates plaid coherence. — Heeger DJ (1987). Model for the extraction of image flow. *J Opt Soc Am A* 4(8):1455–1471. [doi:10.1364/JOSAA.4.001455](https://doi.org/10.1364/josaa.4.001455)
- **MT model:** two stages (V1, then MT). Each computes a **weighted linear sum, then rectification and divisive normalization**. V1 weights give orientation and direction selectivity; MT weights give velocity (speed and direction) selectivity. — Simoncelli EP, Heeger DJ (1998). A model of neuronal responses in visual area MT. *Vision Res* 38(5):743–761. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(97)00183-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(97)00183-1)
- **Fitted cascade (macaque MT):** a linear–nonlinear model operating on a population of nonlinear V1 afferents predicts MT responses to gratings and plaids. Pattern-motion cells have excitatory input from V1 cells with a wide range of preferred directions, **strong motion-opponent suppression**, and tuned normalization. — Rust NC, Mante V, Simoncelli EP, Movshon JA (2006). How MT cells analyze the motion of visual patterns. *Nat Neurosci* 9(11):1421–1431. [doi:10.1038/nn1786](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1786)
- **Speed tuning in MT:** spatiotemporal-frequency response maps have **oriented ridges** of peak sensitivity, as expected for true speed tuning. Preferred speed estimated from the maps matches preferred speed for moving bars. — Perrone JA, Thiele A (2001). Speed skills: measuring the visual speed analyzing properties of primate MT neurons. *Nat Neurosci* 4(5):526–532. [doi:10.1038/87480](https://doi.org/10.1038/87480)
- **Form-invariant speed (macaque MT):** with single sine gratings, **about 25% of MT neurons keep their speed tuning across SF** and 75% show some SF dependence. With two superimposed gratings, or square waves (several SFs moving at the same speed), preferred speed becomes **less SF-dependent**. A speed-tuning nonlinearity boosts responses when several SFs move at the same speed. — Priebe NJ, Cassanello CR, Lisberger SG (2003). The neural representation of speed in macaque area MT/V5. *J Neurosci* 23(13):5650–5661. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.23-13-05650.2003](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.23-13-05650.2003)
- **Speed in V1:** almost all direction-selective simple cells are SF/TF-separable, so their preferred speed falls as SF rises. About a quarter of complex cells are speed-tuned and about a quarter separable. Lower contrast makes complex cells more separable. Speed tuning can be built from separable inputs through spatiotemporal modulation of contrast gain. — Priebe NJ, Lisberger SG, Movshon JA (2006). Tuning for spatiotemporal frequency and speed in directionally selective neurons of macaque striate cortex. *J Neurosci* 26(11):2941–2950. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3936-05.2006](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3936-05.2006)
- **Contrast and perceived speed:** lower-contrast gratings look slower (original report). — Thompson P (1982). Perceived rate of movement depends on contrast. *Vision Res* 22(3):377–380. [doi:10.1016/0042-6989(82)90153-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(82)90153-5)
- **Quantified:** the lower-contrast grating looks slower across 2.5–50% contrast without saturating. On average a **70% contrast grating must be slowed by 35% to match a 10% contrast grating at 2 deg/s (N = 6)**. The effect is roughly linear in log contrast ratio and largely independent of SF. — Stone LS, Thompson P (1992). Human speed perception is contrast dependent. *Vision Res* 32(8):1535–1549. [doi:10.1016/0042-6989(92)90209-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(92)90209-2)
- **SF and perceived speed:** gratings of **higher SF look slower** than lower-SF gratings at the same velocity. Motion sensors in different SF ranges appear to have different velocity transfer functions. — Smith AT, Edgar GK (1990). The influence of spatial frequency on perceived temporal frequency and perceived speed. *Vision Res* 30(10):1467–1474. [doi:10.1016/0042-6989(90)90027-I](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(90)90027-i)
- **Macaque MT at low contrast:** most cells respond less, the **preferred speed of most cells shifts lower**, and about **one third** respond more strongly to slow low-contrast stimuli than to slow high-contrast ones. Labeled-line decoders fed with these responses wrongly predict that lower contrast should make motion look *faster*. — Krekelberg B, van Wezel RJA, Albright TD (2006). Interactions between speed and contrast tuning in the middle temporal area: implications for the neural code for speed. *J Neurosci* 26(35):8988–8998. [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1983-06.2006](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1983-06.2006)
- **Bayesian account:** an estimator that assumes noisy measurements and a **prior favouring slow speeds** accounts for many motion illusions, including contrast-dependent speed. — Weiss Y, Simoncelli EP, Adelson EH (2002). Motion illusions as optimal percepts. *Nat Neurosci* 5(6):598–604. [doi:10.1038/nn0602-858](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn0602-858)
- **Contrast sensitivity of MT:** contrast-response functions get steeper from LGN to MT, and many MT neurons are **more contrast-sensitive than any LGN or V1 cell**. That sensitivity depends on summation across the receptive field. — Sclar G, Maunsell JHR, Lennie P (1990). Coding of image contrast in central visual pathways of the macaque monkey. *Vision Res* 30(1):1–10. [doi:10.1016/0042-6989(90)90123-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(90)90123-3)
- **Review:** MT is full of direction- *and* disparity-selective neurons and is central to motion and depth computations. — Born RT, Bradley DC (2005). Structure and function of visual area MT. *Annu Rev Neurosci* 28:157–189. [doi:10.1146/annurev.neuro.26.041002.131052](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.26.041002.131052)

### Inferences
- **Implementable front end:** x–y–t quadrature Gabor pairs at several SFs and temporal frequencies, squared and summed, then opponent (Adelson–Bergen). Next, MT-like pooling along the speed line in the SF–TF plane, with divisive normalization (Simoncelli–Heeger, Rust et al.). All of this is fixed-weight. (Inference)
- **Robustness to defocus:** defocus removes high-SF energy. Units that compute speed by pooling across SF (Priebe et al. 2003) should still report the right speed from the remaining low-SF channels. Single-SF, separable V1-like units would mis-report speed, because their preferred speed depends on SF. RBC streams are broadband (discrete dark cells) [Assumption], which is the regime in which MT-like speed coding becomes form-invariant. (Inference)
- **Contrast/blur bias to avoid:** blur also lowers effective contrast. Brain-like decoders (a Bayesian slow prior, or labeled lines over MT responses whose tuning shifts at low contrast) would **under-estimate flow in a defocused vessel**. Judging "same flow speed on both sides of a junction" across focused and defocused arms needs contrast-normalized (divisive) responses, or explicit calibration per blur level. (Inference)
- **Direction is more robust than speed:** MT's high, saturating contrast sensitivity suggests direction signals survive low contrast better than speed estimates do. Linking by **direction / flow axis** should therefore be weighted above linking by speed when one arm is defocused. (Inference)

### Gaps
- No primate recording or psychophysics was found that uses **optically defocused moving stimuli** directly. Robustness to blur is inferred from SF-manipulation and contrast studies.
- MT contrast semi-saturation values were not extracted (the Sclar et al. abstract is qualitative).

## 3. Common fate and grouping by motion or temporal coherence (relevant to linking vessel segments that share a flow signal)

### Takeaway
Human vision groups elements that share temporal structure. This holds for synchronized random direction reversals, correlated contrast modulations, common luminance increments or decrements, and synchronized changes in any feature ("messenger"). Temporal structure combines with spatial good continuation **synergistically**, beyond probability summation. Phase delays of about 10–13 ms can segregate figure from ground. Whether *synchrony per se* (as opposed to ordinary early-vision transient and contrast mechanisms) drives grouping is disputed. For a fixed-weight network the low-level explanation is convenient, because temporal-correlation grouping then needs nothing more exotic than temporal filtering and correlation.

### Cited Findings
- **Synchronized reversals:** in an array of non-overlapping apertures, contours move in one of two directions and reverse at random times. Where elements in a region **reverse in synchrony**, the region stands out as a form against unsynchronized surroundings. Clarity depends on reversal rate and on the proportion of synchronized elements. — Lee SH, Blake R (1999). Visual form created solely from temporal structure. *Science* 284(5417):1165–1168. [doi:10.1126/science.284.5417.1165](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.284.5417.1165)
- **Correlated contrast modulation:** in drifting gratings that can be seen either as separate objects or as parts of one object, **temporally correlated contrast modulations increase perceptual coherence** and uncorrelated modulations reduce it. — Alais D, Blake R, Lee SH (1998). Visual features that vary together over time group together over space. *Nat Neurosci* 1(2):160–164. [doi:10.1038/414](https://doi.org/10.1038/414)
- **Presentation synchrony:** grouping improves when the elements of one percept are presented together and temporally separated from other elements on a scale **below the integration time of the visual system**. The authors argue for a global synchrony-based mechanism rather than local motion computation. — Usher M, Donnelly N (1998). Visual synchrony affects binding and segmentation in perception. *Nature* 394(6689):179–182. [doi:10.1038/28166](https://doi.org/10.1038/28166)
- **Contrary evidence:** with a new dynamic stimulus, earlier evidence for synchrony-based grouping can be **explained by well-established early visual mechanisms**, so no synchrony-sensitive binding mechanism needs to be posited. — Farid H, Adelson EH (2001). Synchrony does not promote grouping in temporally structured displays. *Nat Neurosci* 4(9):875–876. [doi:10.1038/nn0901-875](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn0901-875) _(This conflicts with Usher & Donnelly 1998 and with the synchrony interpretation of Lee & Blake 1999.)_
- **Temporal precision:** "colons" flip by 90° at constant intervals, the background flipping in phase and the target with a phase delay, with luminance and apparent-motion artefacts controlled. **Young observers detected targets at delays as short as about 13 ms.** Older observers sometimes needed up to three times longer. — Kandil FI, Fahle M (2001). Purely temporal figure-ground segregation. *Eur J Neurosci* 13(10):2004–2008. [doi:10.1046/j.0953-816x.2001.01573.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.0953-816x.2001.01573.x)
- **Generalized common fate:** elements that **get brighter together or darker together** are grouped. The effect needs common *direction of luminance change over time*, not fixed luminance differences, and is distinct from temporal synchrony. It is described as an "extremely strong" segmentation cue. — Sekuler AB, Bennett PJ (2001). Generalized common fate: grouping by common luminance changes. *Psychol Sci* 12(6):437–444. [doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00382](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00382)
- **Messenger-independence:** observers extract spatial form from synchronized changes in orientation, SF, phase and/or contrast in Gabor arrays. They can combine **different messengers at no cost**, so the visual system abstracts temporal structure regardless of which feature carries it. — Guttman SE, Gilroy LA, Blake R (2005). Mixed messengers, unified message: spatial grouping from temporal structure. *Vision Res* 45(8):1021–1030. [doi:10.1016/j.visres.2004.10.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2004.10.014)
- **Synergy with good continuation ("path finder" Gabor paths):** temporal synchrony of motion-direction changes alone supports path detection when correlation is high. Combining a weak orientation (good-continuation) cue with weak synchrony gives **performance above probability summation**, even though the elements' individual directions were uncorrelated. — Lee SH, Blake R (2001). Neural synergy in visual grouping: when good continuation meets common fate. *Vision Res* 41(16):2057–2064. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(01)00086-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(01)00086-4)
- **Edge-region grouping:** when the edge between two regions groups with one region by common fate, blur similarity, colour, orientation, proximity or **flicker synchrony**, that region tends to be seen as the **closer, figural** one. All six factors worked to varying degrees, and effect strength correlated highly with explicit grouping ratings. — Palmer SE, Brooks JL (2008). Edge-region grouping in figure-ground organization and depth perception. *J Exp Psychol Hum Percept Perform* 34(6):1353–1371. [doi:10.1037/a0012729](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012729)

### Inferences
- **Flow-signal "fingerprints" for linking arms:** [Assumption] RBC flux in microvessels fluctuates (plasma gaps, cell clusters, pulsatility), and these fluctuations travel downstream with the flow. Then the two arms of the *same* vessel on either side of a crossing share a temporal intensity signature, offset by distance divided by velocity. The crossing vessel carries an independent signature. At a bifurcation, parent and daughters share it. **Lagged cross-correlation of arm time series** would pair opposite arms (A↔C, B↔D) at a crossing and give a one-to-two pattern at a branch. This is a computational analogue of grouping by temporal structure (Lee & Blake 1999; Alais et al. 1998). (Inference)
- **Works without resolvable motion:** generalized common fate (Sekuler & Bennett 2001) and messenger-independence (Guttman et al. 2005) suggest a **defocused vessel whose individual RBCs cannot be resolved** can still be linked through shared luminance fluctuations. (Inference)
- **Low-level implementation suffices:** if Farid & Adelson (2001) are right that the effects come from early transient and contrast mechanisms, a fixed-weight network can do this grouping with temporal band-pass filters followed by spatial correlation or pooling. No oscillatory "binding by synchrony" is required. (Inference)
- **Combine cues multiplicatively:** the supra-additive synergy of good continuation and common fate (Lee & Blake 2001) argues for combining **orientation continuity across the junction** with **temporal-signature similarity** conjunctively, for example by multiplying the two terms, rather than averaging them. (Inference)
- **Depth order from edge grouping:** at an overlap, the vessel whose walls (edges) share blur, flicker or common fate with the overlap edges is predicted to be seen as in front (Palmer & Brooks 2008). (Inference)
- A travelling density fluctuation along a vessel is itself a (first- or second-order) motion signal along the vessel axis. Lagged correlation between arms and motion energy along the axis are therefore closely related operations. (Inference)

### Gaps
- No psychophysics was found on grouping by **lagged** (travelling, non-zero-lag) temporal correlation over gaps such as an occluding crossing vessel. The studies retrieved use zero-lag synchrony.
- The synchrony controversy (Usher & Donnelly vs Farid & Adelson) is not resolved in the sources retrieved.

## 4. Kinetic occlusion and accretion/deletion as depth-order cues; occlusion-aware motion integration at T-junctions; structure-from-motion

### Takeaway
Gradual deletion and accretion of texture at an edge specifies that the deleted or accreted surface lies *behind*. Human adults and 5–7-month-old infants use this cue. Motion of texture relative to a boundary ("boundary flow") also orders depth: motion directed into a boundary reads as "behind", shearing motion parallel to it reads as "in front". Motion integration is occlusion-aware. Terminators created by occlusion at T-junctions ("extrinsic") are discounted, and MT neurons represent the implied surface motion behind occluders. Kinetic boundaries are explicitly coded in MT (centre–surround), V2 and V4. Structure-from-motion gives 3-D shape, but for transparent overlapping flows the *depth order* stays ambiguous unless another cue resolves it. MT activity tracks the perceived order.

### Cited Findings
**Accretion/deletion and boundary flow (psychophysics)**
- Kaplan GA (1969). Kinetic disruption of optical texture: the perception of depth at an edge. *Percept Psychophys* 6:193–198. [doi:10.3758/BF03207015](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03207015) (title, volume and pages verified via Crossref; text not retrieved). It is the classic source for Gibson's hypothesis, restated in Granrud et al. (1984, below), that **accretion and deletion of texture gives unambiguous information about which surface is behind at an edge**. The content summary rests on that secondary description, not the original text.
- Gibson JJ, Kaplan GA, Reynolds HN, Wheeler K (1969). The change from visible to invisible: a study of optical transitions. *Percept Psychophys* 5:113–116. [doi:10.3758/BF03210533](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03210533) (bibliographic record verified via Crossref; text not retrieved).
- **Infants (5 and 7 months)**, viewing random-dot displays in which accretion/deletion was the *only* information for contours, reached preferentially for the **apparently nearer (occluding) region**. — Granrud CE, Yonas A, Smith IM, Arterberry ME, Glicksman ML, Sorknes AC (1984). **Occlusion-aware motion integration (T-junction terminators)**
- **Barber-pole (psychophysics):** placing the striped pattern at **uncrossed disparity** relative to the aperture (aperture edges become occluders) **abolishes the disambiguating effect of terminators**. Three apertures separated by stereoscopically "in front" occluding strips are seen as one surface moving vertically. **Extrinsic (occlusion-generated) terminators are treated differently from intrinsic ones.** — Shimojo S, Silverman GH, Nakayama K (1989). **Kinetic-boundary coding**
- **Owl-monkey MT** has columnar segregation. "Interband" neurons have antagonistic surrounds and carry **local motion contrast**, suited to detecting motion boundaries. ### Inferences
- **Accretion/deletion rule for opaque crossings:** if the nearer vessel is optically dense, RBCs in the deeper vessel are *deleted* at the near vessel's walls and *accreted* on the far side, while RBCs in the near vessel cross the overlap continuously. Rule: **the vessel whose particles vanish and reappear at the other vessel's walls is behind.** A fixed-weight detector would compare particle "presence energy" just upstream and downstream of each wall: high temporal-transient energy at a wall where flow terminates indicates deletion. (Inference)
- **Royden-type mapping:** flow inside a vessel runs parallel to its walls, which is *shearing* relative to static tissue, a cue for "vessel in front of background". At a crossing, the deeper vessel's flow is directed *into* the near vessel's walls, which is *occluded motion*, a cue for "behind". The boundary-flow geometry alone therefore predicts depth order, and the human data say the occlusion signal is the stronger of the two. (Inference)
- **Extrinsic versus intrinsic endings:** where a vessel's edges terminate against another vessel through occlusion (T-junctions in the image), the network should treat those terminations as extrinsic. It should continue the deeper vessel amodally across the occluder and test whether matching flow reappears on the far side with similar velocity and temporal signature (Section 3). Real vessel ends and branch points are intrinsic. This mirrors MT's terminator classification (Shimojo et al. 1989; Pack et al. 2004; Duncan et al. 2000). (Inference)
- **Kinetic boundaries define vessel walls:** MT-, V2- or V4-like centre–surround motion-contrast units would mark vessel walls as kinetic boundaries (flowing interior, static exterior), even where luminance contrast is weak because of defocus. (Inference)
- **Transparent (non-occluding) overlaps give no motion-based depth order:** SFM and transparency studies show that two overlapping motions are seen in separate depth planes but in ambiguous order (Bradley et al. 1998; Hiris & Blake 1996). With semi-transparent vessels, order must come from blur (Section 6), accretion/deletion where present, or other cues. (Inference, strongly supported)

### Gaps
- Direct primate single-unit evidence for **accretion/deletion-selective** border-ownership units was not found. Only models (Layton & Yazdanbakhsh 2015; Barnes & Mingolla 2013) and V2/V4 motion-contrast data exist in what was retrieved.
- The texts of Kaplan (1969) and Yonas et al. (1987) were not retrieved, so no quantitative values from them.

## 5. The stream/bounce ambiguity (Metzger 1934; Sekuler, Sekuler & Lau 1997) as a model of crossing-versus-touching decisions, and what disambiguates it

### Takeaway
Two identical targets that move toward each other, overlap and separate can be seen either streaming through each other (a crossing) or bouncing off each other (a contact). With smooth constant-velocity motion and no transients, **streaming dominates**. A transient at coincidence (a sound, flash, pause, disappearance or occlusion) shifts perception toward bouncing. Constant velocity, X-junction cues, local motion coherence, small depth offsets and continuous occluded motion with accretion/deletion favour streaming. Feature-based correspondence across the event needs about 200 ms after coincidence. Sekuler & Sekuler (1999) argue that the governing rules reflect constraints on real 3-D object motion, not a simple directional-recruitment network.

### Cited Findings
- **Origin:** Metzger W (1934). Beobachtungen über phänomenale Identität. *Psychologische Forschung* 19:1–60. [doi:10.1007/BF02409733](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02409733) (record verified via Crossref; text not read).
- **Sound biases toward bouncing:** Sekuler R, Sekuler AB, Lau R (1997). Sound alters visual motion perception. *Nature* 385(6614):308. [doi:10.1038/385308a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/385308a0) (letter; no abstract in PubMed). Later work summarizes the finding: "a brief sound at the moment the targets coincide biases perception toward bouncing" ([Watanabe & Shimojo 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00319)).
- **Baseline:** streaming is the dominant percept in displays free of extra transients, and bouncing predominates when an auditory or visual transient occurs at coincidence. With no sound, streaming was reported on a clear majority of trials regardless of spatial offset. With a tone, bounce reports persisted for vertical offsets up to **17.9 arcmin** and 3-D (depth) offsets up to **25.6 arcmin**, even though those offsets make the trajectories physically consistent with streaming. — Grove PM, Sakurai K (2009). Auditory induced bounce perception persists as the probability of a motion reversal is reduced. *Perception* 38(7):951–965. [doi:10.1068/p5860](https://doi.org/10.1068/p5860)
- **Visual transients and kinematics:** experiments varied temporal transients at coincidence (pausing, disappearance, occlusion), travel distance, and acceleration or deceleration. The tendency to stream was **not related to the strength of a hypothesized directional bias**. The authors conclude that perceived direction reflects neural constraints that mirror the constraints on objects moving in the 3-D world. — Sekuler AB, Sekuler R (1999). Collisions between moving visual targets: what controls alternative ways of seeing an ambiguous display? *Perception* 28(4):415–432. [doi:10.1068/p2909](https://doi.org/10.1068/p2909)
- **Velocity constancy and depth:** two patterns moving in antiphase that superimpose at the centre were more likely to be seen continuing in the same direction (streaming) when moving at **constant rather than variable velocity**. Contradictory *pattern* information reversed this bias only gradually, but a **small amount of contradictory depth information reversed it abruptly**. — Bertenthal BI, Banton T, Bradbury A (1993). Directional bias in the perception of translating patterns. *Perception* 22(2):193–207. [doi:10.1068/p220193](https://doi.org/10.1068/p220193)
- **X-junctions and local motion coherence:** chimpanzees, unlike humans, showed no dominant streaming percept. Depth cues such as **X-junctions and local motion coherence promoted streaming** in them. — Matsuno T, Tomonaga M (2011). Stream/bounce perception and the effect of depth cues in chimpanzees (*Pan troglodytes*). *Atten Percept Psychophys* 73(5):1532–1545. [doi:10.3758/s13414-011-0126-6](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-011-0126-6)
- **Occluded motion and accretion/deletion:** continuous occluded motion near the display strongly biased percepts toward **streaming** even with a sound, over a short occlusion interval of **about 100 ms**. The bias weakened when the occluded motion was interrupted by a spatiotemporal gap or when **deletion/accretion cues were removed**. — Kawachi Y, Gyoba J (2013). Occluded motion alters event perception. *Atten Percept Psychophys* 75(3):491–500. [doi:10.3758/s13414-012-0419-4](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-012-0419-4)
- **Feature correspondence time:** when the targets' colour or luminance was swapped at an occluded coincidence, about **200 ms of post-coincidence viewing** was needed to stabilize stream/bounce judgments based on surface-feature correspondence, similarly across speeds and feature types. — Kawachi Y, Kawabe T, Gyoba J (2011). Stream/bounce event perception reveals a temporal limit of motion correspondence based on surface feature over space and time. *i-Perception* 2(5):428–439. [doi:10.1068/i0399](https://doi.org/10.1068/i0399)
- **Generality:** the effect holds for **luminance- and disparity-defined** targets in dynamic random-element displays. The abstract reports the tone's bounce-promoting window as 250 ms before to 100 ms after coincidence for luminance targets, and 350 ms before to 300 ms after for disparity targets. — Grove PM, Kawachi Y, Sakurai K (2012). The stream/bounce effect occurs for luminance- and disparity-defined motion targets. *Perception* 41(4):379–388. [doi:10.1068/p6808](https://doi.org/10.1068/p6808) _(the PubMed author field reads "Kenzo S"; verify the third author's name form.)_
- **Attention:** distracting attention, either exogenously or endogenously, **increased bouncing** and decreased streaming. Attention may favour seeing object motion continue in its previous direction. — Watanabe K, Shimojo S (1998). Attentional modulation in perception of visual motion events. *Perception* 27(9):1041–1054. [doi:10.1068/p271041](https://doi.org/10.1068/p271041)
- **Auditory context:** the bounce-inducing effect of a sound is weakened by flanking identical sounds 300 ms before and after, through auditory grouping. — Watanabe K, Shimojo S (2001). When sound affects vision: effects of auditory grouping on visual motion perception. *Psychol Sci* 12(2):109–116. [doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00319](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00319)
- **Human fMRI:** with identical stimuli, trials perceived as audio-visually bound (bouncing) showed higher activity in multimodal areas and lower activity in predominantly unimodal areas. — Bushara KO, Hanakawa T, Immisch I, Toma K, Kansaku K, Hallett M (2003). Neural correlates of cross-modal binding. *Nat Neurosci* 6(2):190–195. [doi:10.1038/nn993](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn993)

### Inferences
- **Mapping to vessels:** "streaming" corresponds to *crossing*: each RBC stream keeps its direction and speed through the overlap. "Bouncing" or merging corresponds to *touching/branching*, where trajectories change direction at the junction. Cues that favour the crossing interpretation in humans have direct video analogues: constant velocity through the junction, no transient at the coincidence point, X-junction continuity of both vessels' edges, a depth (blur) difference, and continuous occluded flow with accretion/deletion. (Inference)
- **Implementable "trajectory-continuity" test:** compare motion-energy direction and speed on the entry arm with those on each candidate exit arm, plus temporal signatures (Section 3). Straight-through continuity with matched velocity indicates crossing. Systematic direction change and flux splitting indicate branching. (Inference)
- **Prior:** human vision defaults to streaming (crossing) for smooth, transient-free motion, and abrupt depth information overrides pattern information. By analogy, a network could default to "crossing" at X-shaped overlaps unless flow evidence indicates a branch or anastomosis. Real vessel networks do contain true X-like anastomoses [Assumption], so this prior would need calibration. (Speculation)
- **Time window:** feature-based correspondence needs about 200 ms after coincidence and occlusion bridging works over about 100 ms. For an RBC to be tracked across a junction, the integration window should cover the transit time across the overlap plus about 200 ms. (Inference)

### Gaps
- Quantitative bounce/stream proportions from Sekuler et al. (1997) were not retrieved (no abstract).
- No single-unit primate data on stream/bounce were found. The neural evidence is human fMRI on cross-modal binding (Bushara et al. 2003).
- The attribution of the first stream/bounce description to Metzger (1934) follows the task brief and common usage. The original German text was not read.

## 6. Defocus blur as a depth cue: depth ordering at borders, the sign ambiguity of blur, and neural coding of blur

### Takeaway
Blur differences alone can establish depth order, and the blur of the **occlusion border** is the key. If the border is blurred, the blurrier surface is nearer (it is the occluder). If the border is sharp, the sharper surface is nearer. Humans use this cue inconsistently and coarsely: blur-discrimination Weber fractions are 0.28–0.56, and blur is heavily down-weighted against disparity. When blur is merely rendered on a single display plane, observers fail to use the border rule. They succeed when blur comes from the eye's own optics, partly because **chromatic aberration resolves the near/far sign ambiguity** of defocus. Natural blur statistics bias perception toward "sharp = near, blurred = far". Blur complements disparity: it is more precise away from fixation and conveys absolute scale. Neurally, macaque V4 neurons are jointly tuned to shape and boundary blur. Blur discrimination can be explained by visible contrast energy, without dedicated blur detectors.

### Cited Findings
- **Selective image blur is a pictorial depth cue** even when other pictorial cues are removed. The degree of blur at borders between blurred and sharp regions is used to set the depth order of the regions. — Mather G (1996). Image blur as a pictorial depth cue. *Proc R Soc Lond B* 263(1367):169–172. [doi:10.1098/rspb.1996.0027](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.1996.0027)
- **Occlusion edge blur:** observers judged relative depth in image pairs that differed only in the blur of the boundary between a blurred and a sharp texture region. They **consistently used boundary blur as a relative-depth cue**, but its strength relative to other cues **varied across observers**. The cue can resolve the **near/far ambiguity inherent in depth-from-focus**. — Marshall JA, Burbeck CA, Ariely D, Rolland JP, Martin KE (1996). Occlusion edge blur: a cue to relative visual depth. *J Opt Soc Am A* 13(4):681–688. [doi:10.1364/JOSAA.13.000681](https://doi.org/10.1364/josaa.13.000681)
- **Blur and disparity follow the same geometry:** blur varies with depth along the same course as binocular disparity. Blur discrimination for edges and for fractal textures is similar (they have similar amplitude spectra) and is predicted by **high-SF discrimination**. Stereo works best near fixation, and blur should work better at larger distances. — Mather G (1997). The use of image blur as a depth cue. *Perception* 26(9):1147–1158. [doi:10.1068/p261147](https://doi.org/10.1068/p261147)
- **Cue combination:** adding differential blur to random-dot stereograms changed apparent depth only marginally, and only when the blur was consistent with the sign of the disparity. Combination is **weighted very heavily toward disparity**. — Mather G, Smith DRR (2000). Depth cue integration: stereopsis and image blur. *Vision Res* 40(25):3501–3506. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(00)00178-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(00)00178-4)
- **Coarse cue:** blur variation alone suffices for apparent depth ordering across a sinusoidal border. Blur-discrimination **Weber fractions were 0.28–0.56**, so blur is "a relatively coarse, qualitative depth cue". — Mather G, Smith DRR (2002). Blur discrimination and its relation to blur-mediated depth perception. *Perception* 31(10):1211–1219. [doi:10.1068/p3254](https://doi.org/10.1068/p3254)
- **Border-blur rule and optics (multiplane display):** "If the border is blurred, the blurrier surface is nearer; if the border is sharp, the sharper surface is nearer." With blur **rendered on a single plane**, observers did **not** judge depth order accurately, replicating earlier failures. With the same scenes on **multiple focal planes** (blur produced by the eye's optics), performance was **much better**. **Chromatic aberration** provided useful information and accommodative microfluctuations did not. The authors also note that defocus optics let the eye partly "see around and through occluding objects". — Zannoli M, Love GD, Narain R, Banks MS (2016). Blur and the perception of depth at occlusions. *J Vis* 16(6):17. [doi:10.1167/16.6.17](https://doi.org/10.1167/16.6.17) _(Partly in tension with Marshall et al. 1996, who found consistent though variable use of rendered border blur.)_
- **Sign ambiguity and optimal estimation:** high-precision, unbiased defocus estimates are obtainable from individual natural-image patches with detectable contrast. **Chromatic aberrations fully resolve the sign ambiguity** (nearer versus farther than focus), and monochromatic aberrations resolve it only partially. **Simple spatial and spatio-chromatic receptive fields** extract the defocus information optimally. — Burge J, Geisler WS (2011). Optimal defocus estimation in individual natural images. *PNAS* 108(40):16849–16854. [doi:10.1073/pnas.1108491108](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1108491108)
- **Natural statistics:** blur grows with eccentricity. Blur below the fovea comes mostly from points nearer than fixation and blur above it from points farther. **Large blurs are generally caused by points farther than fixation.** The tendency to see sharp as near and blurred as far matches these statistics. — Sprague WW, Cooper EA, Reissier S, Yellapragada B, Banks MS (2016). The natural statistics of blur. *J Vis* 16(10):23. [doi:10.1167/16.10.23](https://doi.org/10.1167/16.10.23)
- **Absolute distance and scale:** a probabilistic model shows how the pattern of blur, combined with relative depth cues, indicates absolute distance and scale. Viewer data agreed closely with the model for geometrically correct blur and for several approximations. — Held RT, Cooper EA, O'Brien JF, Banks MS (2010). Using blur to affect perceived distance and size. *ACM Trans Graph* 29(2):19. [doi:10.1145/1731047.1731057](https://doi.org/10.1145/1731047.1731057)
- **Complementarity with disparity:** in depth discrimination, disparity was more precise near fixation and **blur more precise away from fixation**. Observers relied on whichever cue was more informative. — Held RT, Cooper EA, Banks MS (2012). Blur and disparity are complementary cues to depth. *Curr Biol* 22(5):426–431. [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2012.01.033](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.01.033)
- **Neural coding (macaque V4):** the first demonstration of blur encoding in primate visual cortex. V4 neurons are tuned to both **object shape and boundary blur**, and the blur tuning is not explained by size, intensity or curvature. In a descriptive model, blur selectivity acts as a separate process that modulates the gain of shape-selective neurons. — Oleskiw TD, Nowack A, Pasupathy A (2018). Joint coding of shape and blur in area V4. *Nat Commun* 9(1):466. [doi:10.1038/s41467-017-02438-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-017-02438-8)
- **No specialized blur mechanism needed for discrimination:** a visible contrast energy (ViCE) model, with masking and the high-frequency limb of the contrast sensitivity function, accounts for the blur-discrimination literature without "intrinsic blur" units or blur-estimation channels. — Watson AB, Ahumada AJ (2011). Blur clarified: a review and synthesis of blur discrimination. *J Vis* 11(5):10. [doi:10.1167/11.5.10](https://doi.org/10.1167/11.5.10)
- **Blur similarity groups edges with regions** and makes the grouped region look closer and figural (one of six edge-region grouping factors). — Palmer & Brooks 2008 ([doi:10.1037/a0012729](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012729)).
- **Human fMRI of blur:** I found only an unpublished thesis (Mussell 2015, University of Nevada, Reno). It reports higher V1 activation for in-focus natural images than for blurred or sharpened versions. This is not peer-reviewed. — [Neural Coding of Image Blur Assessed by fMRI (thesis record)](https://scholarwolf.unr.edu/entities/thesis/b0551404-267a-4904-899e-b3d53ab95ecc)

### Inferences
- **Microscope analogue:** [Assumption] with limited depth of field, the in-focus vessel sits near the focal plane and a defocused vessel is displaced above or below it, with the sign unknown. That is exactly the near/far ambiguity of depth-from-focus. In a **monochrome** camera the chromatic-aberration cue that resolves sign in human vision (Burge & Geisler 2011; Zannoli et al. 2016) is missing, so sign would have to come from occlusion cues, a focus sweep, or asymmetric aberrations. (Speculation)
- **Border-blur rule at opaque crossings:** inspect the boundary of the overlap patch, which is formed by the near vessel's walls running across the far vessel. A **sharp** boundary means the sharp vessel is in front; a **blurred** boundary means the blurry vessel is in front. This can be computed from local edge-blur estimates using fixed filters: simple receptive fields suffice per Burge & Geisler, and contrast-energy ratios across SF bands per Watson & Ahumada. (Inference)
- **Transparent (absorptive) overlaps:** if both vessels remain visible through the overlap, both sets of walls persist and the border-blur rule may not apply. Blur would then identify *which arms belong to which vessel*, not depth order. Depth order may be unrecoverable from blur alone, as with SFM ambiguity in Section 4. (Speculation)
- **Blur as a linking/grouping cue:** a vessel running at roughly constant depth keeps roughly constant blur along its length, so blur continuity across a junction links opposite arms (a crossing). The two vessels at a crossing typically differ in blur, while branch daughters share the parent's depth at the branch point. This parallels blur similarity as a grouping factor (Palmer & Brooks 2008). (Inference)
- **Machine can beat humans here:** human blur discrimination is coarse (Weber 0.28–0.56), but optimal defocus estimation from natural patches is precise (Burge & Geisler 2011). A network with explicit defocus-estimation filters could use much smaller blur differences than humans do. (Inference)
- **Interaction with motion processing:** defocus moves a vessel's motion energy to low-SF channels. Combined with SF-specific opponency (Section 1), blur thus also *separates* the transparent flows of a sharp and a blurred vessel. (Inference)

### Gaps
- Neural coding of blur **sign** (near versus far of focus) in primate cortex: no study found.
- No peer-reviewed human fMRI mapping of blur-selective responses was found. The only source is an unpublished thesis.
- No study was found on blur-based depth ordering for **transparent or semi-transparent** overlapping structures, as opposed to opaque occlusion.
- Marshall et al. (1996) and Zannoli et al. (2016) disagree on how well rendered (non-optical) border blur is used. Zannoli et al. attribute the difference to optics, including chromatic aberration.

## 7. Binocular disparity at junctions (da Vinci stereopsis) — brief contrast case

### Takeaway
Disparity is the strongest depth-order cue at overlaps. It rescues motion transparency, makes MT inhibition depth-specific, converts terminators to extrinsic, flips stream/bounce abruptly, and disambiguates transparent depth order. Monocular unpaired regions near occluding edges (da Vinci stereopsis) also signal depth and create subjective occluding contours. All of this needs two views, which single-objective microscopy lacks. In the specific role of separating channels for transparency, defocus is the closest monocular analogue.

### Cited Findings
- **da Vinci stereopsis:** unpaired image points, visible to one eye only beside an occluding surface, give perceived depth that **increases with horizontal separation from the fused edge over about 25–40 arcmin**. They also produce subjective occluding contours of the appropriate depth sign. The process needs eye-of-origin information, possibly from V1 onward. — Nakayama K, Shimojo S (1990). da Vinci stereopsis: depth and subjective occluding contours from unpaired image points. *Vision Res* 30(11):1811–1825. [doi:10.1016/0042-6989(90)90161-D](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(90)90161-d)
- Disparity differences restore transparency in locally balanced displays (Qian et al. 1994, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.14-12-07357.1994)). MT opponent inhibition operates mainly within a disparity (Bradley et al. 1995, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/373609a0)). Stereo disambiguates transparent depth order but does not change direction repulsion (Hiris & Blake 1996, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0952523800007227)). Uncrossed disparity converts barber-pole terminators to extrinsic (Shimojo et al. 1989, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(89)90047-3)). A small amount of depth information abruptly reverses the stream bias (Bertenthal et al. 1993, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1068/p220193)). Disparity-defined bistable SFM is coded in MT (Dodd et al. 2001, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.21-13-04809.2001)).
- Disparity dominates blur when both are present (Mather & Smith 2000, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(00)00178-4)). Blur and disparity share geometric constraints but differ in where they are precise (Held et al. 2012, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.01.033); Mather 1997, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1068/p261147)).

### Inferences
- In monocular microscopy video, the junction-relevant *roles* of disparity are depth-specific opponency, extrinsic-terminator classification and depth-order assignment. A network would need to fill these with defocus (Section 6) and kinetic occlusion (Section 4). Because blur and disparity share geometry (Mather 1997; Held et al. 2012), a "defocus-specific" opponency stage is a plausible stand-in for MT's disparity-specific opponency. (Speculation)

### Gaps
- No studies were found that test defocus as a *substitute* for disparity in rescuing motion transparency. Qian et al.'s SF-content manipulation is the closest evidence.

## 8. Perceiving flow in vessels or particle streams, coherent motion along curved paths, and motion along contours

### Takeaway
No primate vision research was found on seeing blood flow *per se*, apart from the blue-field entoptic phenomenon, in which people see their own retinal leukocytes moving. The closest psychophysics is on detecting single-particle **trajectories in motion noise**. Such trajectories are detected far better than local motion detectors predict, tolerate curvature, and benefit from integration over 250–600 ms. This points to a fixed-weight network that facilitates direction-tuned units along their preferred direction. Motion directed **along** a contour may be easier to detect than other directions, but this is contested. Fast motion produces oriented "motion streaks" coded in early visual cortex. Form cues that imply a path (Glass patterns) drive motion areas and bias perceived direction, linking vessel orientation to flow direction. Liquids can be recognized from motion statistics alone.

### Cited Findings
- **Blood-flow perception (entoptic):** Riva CE, Petrig B (1980). Blue field entoptic phenomenon and blood velocity in the retinal capillaries. *J Opt Soc Am* 70(10):1234–1238. [doi:10.1364/JOSA.70.001234](https://doi.org/10.1364/josa.70.001234). PubMed indexing (MeSH: Blood Flow Velocity, Capillaries, Leukocytes, Perception, Retinal Vessels) shows it relates perceived moving entoptic corpuscles to retinal capillary blood velocity. No abstract was available.
- **Trajectory in noise:** a single dot moving on a trajectory among random-direction (Brownian) noise dots with identical step size and frame rate is detected at up to **90% when the nearest-neighbour mismatch probability is 0.3**. Detection improves with duration up to **250–400 ms**. It survives direction changes of **about 30° or less per frame**, and low-curvature circular paths are as detectable as straight ones. The authors attribute this to facilitation between similarly tuned detectors stimulated in sequence along their preferred direction. — Watamaniuk SNJ, McKee SP, Grzywacz NM (1995). Detecting a trajectory embedded in random-direction motion noise. *Vision Res* 35(1):65–77. [doi:10.1016/0042-6989(94)E0047-O](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(94)e0047-o)
- **Beyond local detectors:** local motion-energy detectors with a max-rule decision explain brief (100 ms) trajectories but **underestimate extended ones**. A **200 ms trajectory is about three times more detectable than two isolated 100 ms trajectories**. Circular trajectories (the abstract specifies curvatures larger than 1 degree) are almost as detectable as linear ones. The data fit a network that feeds excitation forward among units tuned to similar directions. — Verghese P, Watamaniuk SNJ, McKee SP, Grzywacz NM (1999). Local motion detectors cannot account for the detectability of an extended trajectory in noise. *Vision Res* 39(1):19–30. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(98)00033-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(98)00033-9)
- **Temporal-coherence theory (an implementable network):** a special-purpose network makes local motion signals coherent in space and time, boosting features that move along non-random trajectories. Detection improves over durations of at least **600 ms** and worsens with eccentricity. — Grzywacz NM, Watamaniuk SNJ, McKee SP (1995). Temporal coherence theory for the detection and measurement of visual motion. *Vision Res* 35(22):3183–3203. [doi:10.1016/0042-6989(95)00102-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(95)00102-6)
- **Motion along contours:** in a path-paradigm variant, contours whose local motion runs **along the contour** were more detectable than contours with any common direction. Straight and moderately curved contours supported this, highly curved ones did not. — Ledgeway T, Hess RF (2002). Rules for combining the outputs of local motion detectors to define simple contours. *Vision Res* 42(5):653–659. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(01)00247-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(01)00247-4)
- **Contrary evidence:** visibility of moving contours **did not depend** on motion direction relative to contour orientation unless the contour had to pass through fixation. The authors suggest the collinear advantage reflects element-detection probability. Visibility was invariant to element SF but depended on speed, element number and spacing, and on angle and SF differences between neighbours. — Bex PJ, Simmers AJ, Dakin SC (2003). Grouping local directional signals into moving contours. *Vision Res* 43(20):2141–2153. [doi:10.1016/S0042-6989(03)00329-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(03)00329-8) _(This contradicts Ledgeway & Hess 2002.)_
- **Motion streaks:** temporal integration smears fast features into streaks oriented along the motion. Orientation masking and adaptation show these signals exist for speeds **above about 1 feature width per 100 ms**, and simulations show they can determine direction in natural images. — Geisler WS (1999). Motion streaks provide a spatial code for motion direction. *Nature* 400(6739):65–69. [doi:10.1038/21886](https://doi.org/10.1038/21886)
- **Human fMRI:** fast motion raised thresholds for static orientations *parallel* to the motion, and slow motion raised them for orthogonal orientations. A classifier trained on early-visual-cortex activity for static orientations decoded the direction of **fast ("streaky") but not slow** motion. — Apthorp D, Schwarzkopf DS, Kaul C, Bahrami B, Alais D, Rees G (2013). Direct evidence for encoding of motion streaks in human visual cortex. *Proc R Soc B* 280(1752):20122339. [doi:10.1098/rspb.2012.2339](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2012.2339)
- **Implied motion (macaque STS plus human psychophysics):** STS motion-area cells respond to dynamic Glass patterns, which have no coherent motion but suggest a motion path. When motion and form cues conflict, humans and monkeys perceive a **compromise direction**, and STS cells shift their direction preferences accordingly. — Krekelberg B, Dannenberg S, Hoffmann KP, Bremmer F, Ross J (2003). Neural correlates of implied motion. *Nature* 424(6949):674–677. [doi:10.1038/nature01852](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01852)
- Sequences of Glass patterns built to a common global form rule, with **no coherent velocity signals**, produce perceived motion consistent with the form. — Ross J, Badcock DR, Hayes A (2000). Coherent global motion in the absence of coherent velocity signals. *Curr Biol* 10(11):679–682. [doi:10.1016/S0960-9822(00)00524-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0960-9822(00)00524-8)
- **Liquids from motion:** observers recognize liquids and judge viscosity from noise-patch motion fields alone. **Local speed** is critical for apparent viscosity. **Spatial smoothness statistics**, including the mean discrete Laplacian of motion vectors, matter for the impression of liquidness. — Kawabe T, Maruya K, Fleming RW, Nishida S (2015). Seeing liquids from visual motion. *Vision Res* 109:125–138. [doi:10.1016/j.visres.2014.07.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2014.07.003)

### Inferences
- **RBC streams are "trajectories in noise":** [Assumption] in stabilized vessel video, each RBC traces a path along the vessel axis, often among clutter and noise. A Grzywacz/Watamaniuk-type temporal-coherence network would enhance these paths. In fixed-weight form this is a space-time association field in which direction-tuned units excite neighbours displaced along their preferred direction. It tolerates moderate curvature (about 30° per frame or less, in human data) and benefits from 250–600 ms of integration. (Inference)
- **Co-alignment of form and flow:** flow inside a vessel is parallel to the vessel axis. Ledgeway & Hess (2002) and the implied-motion findings (Krekelberg et al. 2003) motivate pairing each vessel's orientation channel with the motion component along that axis. At a crossing, each transparent component can then be **assigned to the vessel whose axis it parallels**. Bex et al. (2003) caution that a perceptual collinearity advantage is not firmly established, but the image-statistics argument holds regardless. (Inference)
- **Streaks as orientation reinforcement:** fast RBCs (above about one cell diameter per 100 ms, in human integration terms) produce streak orientation energy aligned with the vessel. This lets static-orientation filters detect a vessel's axis and flow axis even when single cells are not resolved, for example in a defocused vessel. (Inference)
- **Motion-field smoothness:** junctions (crossings or branches) should be local spikes in motion-field roughness, such as the Laplacian of the velocity field, inside otherwise smooth intravascular flow. This is a candidate fixed-filter junction detector inspired by Kawabe et al. (2015). (Speculation)

### Gaps
- No human or primate psychophysics was found on **crossing-versus-branching judgments in tube networks** with internal particle flow, or on how clinicians or observers perceive RBC flow in microscopy (capillaroscopy, intravital) video.
- No abstract of Riva & Petrig (1980) was available, so no quantitative values.
- Ledgeway & Hess (2002) and Bex et al. (2003) conflict on whether motion along a contour is advantaged.
