## 1. Junction typology (T, L, X, Y/fork, arrow, psi) and evidence that junctions carry disproportionate shape/depth information

### Takeaway
Junction catalogues (Guzmán; Huffman–Clowes; Waltz) map each junction type to a small set of physically possible 3-D interpretations. T = occlusion, X = transparency or crossing, Y/arrow = a convex or concave vertex. Behavioural evidence that junctions and curvature extrema carry extra information is fairly strong for scenes (Walther & Shen 2014, a causal manipulation) and for objects (Biederman's vertex deletion, Attneave). Two caveats matter for vessel work. Dissenting studies exist. And McDermott (2004) showed that in real images most junctions cannot be classified from a small local window: observers needed apertures of about 50–100 px.

### Cited Findings
**Computational line-labelling catalogues.** These are formal theories, not psychophysics, but they are the source of the explicit junction→interpretation tables.
- Guzmán (1968) segmented line drawings of polyhedral scenes into bodies. His program used local vertex types (L, fork/Y, arrow, T, X, peak, etc.) as "links" between regions: forks and arrows vote that adjacent regions belong to one body, and T-junctions vote that the stem region is occluded by the bar region. Guzmán, A. (1968). Decomposition of a visual scene into three-dimensional bodies. *AFIPS Fall Joint Computer Conference*, 33, 291–304 — [DOI 10.1145/1476589.1476631](https://doi.org/10.1145/1476589.1476631) [P]
- Huffman and Clowes, working independently, labelled the edges of trihedral polyhedra as convex (+), concave (−) or occluding (arrow). Only a small catalogue of junction labellings is physically realizable: the commonly cited count is 18 legal labellings across L, fork, arrow and T, out of 208 combinatorial possibilities. A drawing is interpretable only if a consistent labelling exists. Sources: Huffman, D. A. (1971). Impossible objects as nonsense sentences. *Machine Intelligence*, 6, 295–323 (Edinburgh Univ. Press); Clowes, M. B. (1971). On seeing things. *Artificial Intelligence*, 2(1), 79–116 — [DOI 10.1016/0004-3702(71)90005-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702(71)90005-1) [P]
- Waltz (1975) extended the labels to shadows and cracks, which produced thousands of junction labellings. He showed that local constraint propagation ("Waltz filtering") prunes the candidates to near-unique interpretations. Waltz, D. (1975). Understanding line drawings of scenes with shadows. In P. H. Winston (Ed.), *The Psychology of Computer Vision* (pp. 19–91). McGraw-Hill (no online link verified) [P]
- Malik (1987) extended the catalogue to curved (piecewise-smooth) objects. Malik, J. (1987). Interpreting line drawings of curved objects. *International Journal of Computer Vision*, 1, 73–103 — [DOI 10.1007/BF00128527](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00128527) [P]

**Curvature extrema and vertices as information carriers (objects).**
- Attneave (1954) argued that shape information is concentrated where contour direction changes most (curvature maxima, angles). Observers asked to approximate a shape with a fixed number of dots placed them preferentially at curvature maxima. His "sleeping cat", drawn by joining points of maximum curvature with straight lines, remains recognizable. Attneave, F. (1954). Some informational aspects of visual perception. *Psychological Review*, 61(3), 183–193 — [DOI 10.1037/h0054663](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0054663) [P]. Status: classic, much cited. Formalized as a curvature/surprisal relation in Feldman, J., & Singh, M. (2005). Information along contours and object boundaries. *Psychological Review*, 112(1), 243–252 — [DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.112.1.243](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.112.1.243) [P]
- Conflicting evidence: Kennedy & Domander (1985) reported that fragmented outlines retaining the points of maximum change were *less* useful for recognition than fragments taken elsewhere. Kennedy, J. M., & Domander, R. (1985). Shape and contour: The points of maximum change are least useful for recognition. *Perception*, 14, 367–370 — [DOI 10.1068/p140367](https://doi.org/10.1068/p140367) [P]. The later literature cites this as a small-sample challenge to vertex/curvature-extremum primacy — [Panis et al. 2007 (KU Leuven PDF)](https://fac.ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Panis2007IOEOO.pdf) [V]
- Biederman's recognition-by-components theory treats L, fork, arrow and T vertices as non-accidental properties that specify geon structure, with T-junctions signalling occlusion. Biederman, I. (1987). Recognition-by-components: A theory of human image understanding. *Psychological Review*, 94(2), 115–147 — [DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.94.2.115](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.94.2.115) [P]
- Vertex-deletion experiment (Biederman & Blickle, 1985, unpublished, reported in Biederman 1987). Line drawings of 18 objects had 25%, 45% or 65% of their contour deleted, either at vertices or at midsegments. At 100 ms exposure with 65% deletion, vertex deletion produced higher error rates than midsegment deletion. Exact percentages were not retrievable this session — [secondary summary, arXiv 2306.04955](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.04955); [Panis et al. 2007](https://fac.ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Panis2007IOEOO.pdf) [V]. In the same paper, Biederman reported that "non-recoverable" deletions, which remove vertices so that the component structure cannot be restored, made objects largely unidentifiable [P].
- Replication/dissent: deleting vertices was more detrimental than deleting midsegments for letter and word identification. Lanthier, S. N., Risko, E. F., Stolz, J. A., & Besner, D. (2009). *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* — [PDF](https://uwaterloo.ca/besner-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/lanthier_risko_stolz_besner_pbr_2009.pdf) [V]. By contrast, a 1999 *Perceptual & Motor Skills* study (N = 169) found no significant difference between intact-vertex and random-fragmentation pictures — [DOI 10.2466/pms.1999.88.3.983](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.1999.88.3.983) (via secondary summary) [V]. Status: the direction of the vertex effect is replicated in several labs, but its size depends on the task.

**Junctions in natural-scene line drawings (Walther and colleagues).**
- Walther & Shen (2014) extracted X, T, Y and arrow junction types and junction angles from artist line drawings of natural scenes. L-junctions were excluded "for practical reasons." They also measured contour orientation, length and curvature. In rapid categorization (SOA staircased to 65% correct, mean 26.5 ms), human error patterns correlated best with junction angles (r = 0.730), then curvature (r = 0.583), then junction types (r = 0.513); orientation and length were not significant. In Experiment 2, every contour was randomly translated, which keeps contour-level properties but destroys junctions. Human accuracy fell from 53.8% to 31.9% (t(22) = 9.26, p = 4.8×10⁻⁹), and junction-based error correlations disappeared. SVM decoding from junction-type counts fell from 47.4% to 35.2%. Walther, D. B., & Shen, D. (2014). Nonaccidental properties underlie human categorization of complex natural scenes. *Psychological Science*, 25(4), 851–860 — [DOI 10.1177/0956797613512662](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613512662); [PMC3984348](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3984348) [V]. Status: a single study, but with a causal manipulation; extended by fMRI work (next item).
- Choo & Walther (2016) reported that contour-junction statistics underlie neural representations of scene categories in high-level visual cortex (fMRI with manipulated line drawings) — [bioRxiv 10.1101/044156](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/044156v2) [V]

**Are junctions locally detectable in real images?**
- McDermott (2004), paradigm: one group of observers labelled every occlusion point in real images. A second group saw circular apertures of 13, 25, 49, 97 or 201 px diameter and judged whether each was centred on an occlusion point (40% real occlusion points, 45% random high-variance patches, plus synthetic L-, T- and edge controls). At 13 px, observers misclassified 27% of patches (chance = 50%). Only just over half of the true occlusion points were recognized, rising to over 90% at the largest aperture. Errors did not reach their minimum until about 100 px, and false positives stayed roughly constant across sizes. Synthetic T-junctions were recognized at almost all aperture sizes. Blur-and-subsample controls ruled out a simple "junctions at a coarser scale" explanation. Conclusion: "although some junctions in real images are locally defined and can be detected with simple mechanisms, a substantial fraction necessitate the use of more complex and global processes." McDermott, J. (2004). Psychophysics with junctions in real images. *Perception*, 33, 1101–1127 — [DOI 10.1068/p5265](https://doi.org/10.1068/p5265); [PDF](https://web.mit.edu/jhm/www/Pubs/McDermott_2004_junction_psychophysics.pdf) [V]. Status: a single study (5 observers, about 700 trials each), but uses natural images.
- Junction type is not always decisive for motion interpretation either. Changing junctions from T to L sometimes had large and sometimes little effect on perceived motion of a cross moving behind an aperture. McDermott, J., & Adelson, E. H. (2004). Junctions and cost functions in motion interpretation. *Journal of Vision*, 4(7), 552–563 — [abstract](https://persci.mit.edu/pub_abstracts/McDermott_jov-4-7-3.html) [V]. See also McDermott, Weiss & Adelson (2001). Beyond junctions: nonlocal form constraints on motion interpretation. *Perception*, 30, 905–923 — [DOI 10.1068/p3219](https://doi.org/10.1068/p3219) [V]

**Are junctions/intersections pre-attentive features?**
- Julesz proposed line terminators and crossings as "textons". T-among-L discrimination required serial processing, whereas +-among-L appeared parallel. Julesz, B. (1981). Textons, the elements of texture perception, and their interactions. *Nature*, 290, 91–97 — [DOI 10.1038/290091a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/290091a0); Bergen, J. R., & Julesz, B. (1983). Parallel versus serial processing in rapid pattern discrimination. *Nature*, 303, 696–698 — [DOI 10.1038/303696a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/303696a0) [P]
- Wolfe & DiMase (2003) argued that the apparent pop-out of intersections (e.g. + among L) is confounded by size and line terminators. With these controlled, search for intersections was "very inefficient", so intersection should not be counted as a basic visual-search feature. The X-vs-T distinction is likewise not picked up pre-attentively. Wolfe, J. M., & DiMase, J. S. (2003). Do intersections serve as basic features in visual search? *Perception*, 32(6), 645–656 — [DOI 10.1068/p3414](https://doi.org/10.1068/p3414) [V]
- Search among line drawings of blocks can be efficient when the targets differ in implied 3-D orientation through their Y/arrow junction structure. Enns, J. T., & Rensink, R. A. (1991). Preattentive recovery of three-dimensional orientation from line drawings. *Psychological Review*, 98(3), 335–351 — [DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.98.3.335](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.98.3.335) [P]

### Inferences
- For a fixed-weight vessel network, the line-labelling tradition suggests a two-stage design. First, compute local junction descriptors: number of arms, inter-arm angles, which arm pairs are collinear, and luminance ordering of the sectors. Second, apply a constraint-propagation step along the vessel graph (Waltz-style) to remove interpretations that are locally possible but globally inconsistent. Example: a vessel cannot be "artery" on one side of a crossing and "vein" on the continuation.
- McDermott (2004) implies that windows of roughly one vessel diameter will misclassify many real junctions. Descriptors should integrate arm orientation, width and contrast over several vessel widths along each arm. This matches the large-aperture requirement, though converting pixels to vessel widths is my own extrapolation.
- Wolfe & DiMase (2003) suggest that humans do not detect X-vs-T "for free". Junction type is computed by a relational, serial-like operation on arms, which supports explicit arm-pairing computations over a single "junction detector" filter.
- Walther & Shen's finding that junction *angle* outweighs junction *type* suggests encoding continuous angles, not just discrete labels.

### Gaps
- No psychophysical study specifically on **psi (ψ) junctions** was found this session. They appear mainly in computational lightness and shadow work.
- The exact Biederman & Blickle (1985) error percentages were not retrievable; only the direction of the effect is verified.
- Huffman–Clowes, Waltz and Guzmán are machine-vision theories. They have not been validated as models of human junction classification in natural images.

## 2. Good continuation across crossings and branch points: contour integration and the association field

### Takeaway
Humans link oriented elements into contours according to an "association field". Linking is strongest for elements that are nearby, co-oriented and co-circular. It declines gradually with path angle and then abruptly for very curved paths; objective tasks show essentially no linking beyond about 90°. Linking is largely insensitive to contrast polarity. The field's shape matches edge co-occurrence statistics in natural images, which gives a principled, non-learned weight set. No study found this session directly measured integration of Gabor paths that cross or branch. The X/Y rule therefore has to be inferred: at an X, pair the arms with the smallest turning angle.

### Cited Findings
- Field, Hayes & Hess (1993), paradigm: paths of about 10–12 Gabor elements embedded among randomly oriented Gabors, in 2AFC detection. Element orientation relative to the path, the angle between neighbours ("path angle"), and element separation were varied. Detection depended strongly on relative orientation and spacing, and elements aligned along the path ("snakes") were best. The authors proposed a local association field: facilitation is strongest for neighbours that are close in position and orientation along a smooth path, with inhibition off-axis. Field, D. J., Hayes, A., & Hess, R. F. (1993). Contour integration by the human visual system: evidence for a local "association field." *Vision Research*, 33(2), 173–193 — [DOI 10.1016/0042-6989(93)90156-Q](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(93)90156-Q) (DOI suffix confirmed in reference list of [Carrigan et al. 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf)); summary in [Hess, May & Dumoulin 2014 review](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18317/1/Hess_May_Dumoulin_2013.pdf) [V]. Status: robust, replicated in many labs.
- Path-angle dependence (Hess et al. review of Field et al. data): "Performance remains relatively good for paths of intermediate curvature but declines abruptly once the path becomes very curved." Smooth curves (constant sign of turn) are detected slightly better than jagged ones but show the same curvature dependence. A single elongated linear filter predicts a much steeper curvature fall-off than humans show, implying linking across an array of units rather than spatial summation — [Hess, May & Dumoulin 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18317/1/Hess_May_Dumoulin_2013.pdf) [V]
- Objective performance tasks, including Field et al. (1993), "tend not to show interpolation effects beyond 90 degrees." Subjective-report and priming methods sometimes show weak effects slightly beyond 90° — [Carrigan, Palmer & Kellman 2016, JEP:HPP](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf), DOI [10.1037/xhp0000233](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000233) [V]
- Element arrangement matters: snakes (elements along the path) are easiest, ladders (perpendicular) harder, and ropes (all oblique) essentially undetectable. This happens even though an ideal observer would perform identically on all three, so it reflects mechanism, not stimulus information. Ladder detection declines faster with eccentricity, modelled with a crowding-type integration field (May & Hess 2007) — [Hess, May & Dumoulin 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18317/1/Hess_May_Dumoulin_2013.pdf) [V]
- Polarity: reversing the contrast polarity of alternate path elements (and half the background) affects contour detection "only marginally" (Field, Hayes & Hess 1997, as cited), defeating any linear elongated-receptive-field account — [Hess, May & Dumoulin 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18317/1/Hess_May_Dumoulin_2013.pdf) [V]. Status: replicated.
- Natural-image statistics as fixed weights: an association field derived from edge co-occurrence statistics in natural images "accurately accounted for human data on a contour detection task" (Geisler et al. 2001). Elder & Goldberg (2002) followed with a similar ecological approach — [Hess, May & Dumoulin 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18317/1/Hess_May_Dumoulin_2013.pdf) [V]. Primary sources: Geisler, W. S., Perry, J. S., Super, B. J., & Gallogly, D. P. (2001). Edge co-occurrence in natural images predicts contour grouping performance. *Vision Research*, 41(6), 711–724 — [DOI 10.1016/S0042-6989(00)00277-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0042-6989(00)00277-7); Elder, J. H., & Goldberg, R. M. (2002). Ecological statistics of Gestalt laws for the perceptual organization of contours. *Journal of Vision*, 2(4), 5 (pp. 324–353) — [DOI 10.1167/2.4.5](https://doi.org/10.1167/2.4.5) [V for citation; P for DOIs]
- An ideal observer using the generating association field outperforms humans. Even the single best-fitting association field across all conditions outperformed human observers. Ernst, U. A., et al. (2012). Optimality of human contour integration. *PLoS Computational Biology*, 8(5) — cited in [Hess, May & Dumoulin 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18317/1/Hess_May_Dumoulin_2013.pdf) [V]
- Closure: closed contours are detected far more easily than open ones of the same length. Kovács, I., & Julesz, B. (1993). A closed curve is much more than an incomplete one: Effect of closure in figure-ground segmentation. *PNAS*, 90, 7495–7497 — [DOI 10.1073/pnas.90.16.7495](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.90.16.7495) [V for citation; P for effect size]
- Gestalt origin: Wertheimer's "good continuation" was introduced for segmenting fully visible displays of "continuous, branching lines." The classic demonstration is an X seen as two crossing lines rather than two touching V's — [Carrigan, Palmer & Kellman 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf) [V]
- In Field et al.-type path detection, relatable Gabor elements group and "pop out", yet no continuous illusory contour is seen. Grouping and contour completion can therefore dissociate — [Carrigan, Palmer & Kellman 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf) [V]
- Bayesian formulations treat successive turning angles as drawn from a distribution peaked at 0° (straight continuation), so grouping likelihood falls with turning angle. Feldman, J. (2001). Bayesian contour integration. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 63(7), 1171–1182 (DOI not verified) [P]

### Inferences
- **Explicit rule for X vs Y/T.** Score every pair of arms meeting at a junction with an association-field weight, w(Δθ, d) ≈ exp(−Δθ²/2σθ²)·exp(−d²/2σd²), where Δθ is the turning angle between an incoming arm and the candidate continuation. A Geisler-style co-occurrence table is the empirically grounded alternative.
  - **Crossing (X):** two disjoint arm pairs each have small turning angles. From the psychophysics, good linking holds up to roughly 30–45° and collapses well before 90°.
  - **Bifurcation (Y):** at most one arm pair is near-collinear, with the parent continuing into the larger daughter, or no pair is collinear (symmetric fork).
  - **T-like:** one collinear pair plus an arm that terminates on it.
  - The exact σθ is an engineering choice. Field et al. type data constrain it to well under 90°, but precise numbers were not verified this session (see Gaps).
- Because contour linking is polarity-insensitive (Field et al. 1997) but transparency is polarity-sensitive (Section 5), a fixed network can separate the two: compute geometric continuation with polarity-invariant (energy) units, and use signed-contrast units only for the transparency/occlusion test.
- Ladders and ropes are poorly integrated. Vessel *edges* (two parallel boundaries) form snake-like paths, so linking should run along edge or centreline orientation, not across the vessel.

### Gaps
- **The exact path-angle thresholds of Field et al. (1993) could not be verified from the primary text this session.** Path angles of about 0–90° were tested; the common secondary summary is good performance up to about 30°, a steep decline by about 60°, and near chance by about 90°. Treat these values as unverified.
- **No study found** that directly measured contour integration when the target path *crosses* or *branches through* another path or a distractor contour (e.g. X- or Y-shaped Gabor paths). The good-continuation rule at crossings rests on Gestalt demonstrations and on the general association-field data, not on dedicated crossing experiments.
- All association-field psychophysics used Gabor or line stimuli. Only Geisler et al. (2001) and Elder & Goldberg (2002) tie the weights to natural-image statistics, and those natural images were not vascular.

## 3. Curve tracing: speed, effects of nearby curves and crossings, visual routines, and branch choice at X vs Y

### Takeaway
Deciding whether two points lie on the same curve takes time that grows with distance *along the curve*. This is attributed to a serial "tracing" routine (Ullman 1984), implemented as object-based attention spreading along the curve. Tracing slows when other curves are nearby: the operator's spatial scale appears limited by the distance to the nearest distractor ("zoom lens", "growth cone"). Crossings, especially at small angles, slow path following and cause back-and-forth eye movements (graph-reading studies). No study found directly measured how observers pick the continuing branch at an X vs a Y.

### Cited Findings
- Ullman (1984) proposed "visual routines": serial compositions of elemental operations (shifting the processing focus, indexing, bounded activation/"coloring", boundary tracing, marking). They compute spatial relations such as inside/outside or same-curve that parallel feature maps cannot compute. Ullman, S. (1984). Visual routines. *Cognition*, 18, 97–159 — [DOI 10.1016/0010-0277(84)90023-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(84)90023-4) [P]
- Jolicoeur, Ullman & MacKay (1986), paradigm: judge whether two dots lie on the same curve, with Euclidean distance controlled. Time increased monotonically, but nonlinearly, with distance along the curve, which is the main evidence for mental curve tracing. Jolicoeur, P., Ullman, S., & MacKay, M. (1986). Curve tracing: A possible basic operation in the perception of spatial relations. *Memory & Cognition*, 14(2), 129–140 — [DOI 10.3758/BF03198373](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198373) [P for bibliographic details]; finding summarized in [Springer abstract, DOI 10.3758/BF03199570](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03199570) [V]. Status: robust, replicated (e.g. Pringle & Egeth 1988; Kwak and colleagues).
- Tracing time scales with curve distance *relative to overall pattern size*, not absolute distance (size invariance). Tracing rate is set by stimulus properties that scale with the pattern, notably "the distance between the traced curve and nearby distractor curves" and the curvature of the traced curve — [Springer, DOI 10.3758/BF03198493](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03198493) (Jolicoeur & Ingleton 1991, *Memory & Cognition*, "Size invariance in curve tracing" — authors and title [P]) [V for the finding]
- Zoom-lens operator: distractors that constrain the operator's receptive field produce roughly linear distance effects. Distractors that leave room for a larger field produce nonlinear (faster) tracing over those segments. A zoom-lens model fit best — [Springer, DOI 10.3758/BF03199570](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03199570) (McCormick & Jolicoeur 1991, *Memory & Cognition* — authors [P]) [V for the finding]. The same abstract cites Jolicoeur, Ullman & MacKay (1991), "Visual curve tracing properties", *JEP:HPP*, 17(4), 997–1022 — [DOI 10.1037/0096-1523.17.4.997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.17.4.997) [P for DOI], which proposed a bipartite tracing operator and reported slowing with nearby distractors.
- Houtkamp, Spekreijse & Roelfsema (2003) showed a gradual spread of object-based attention along the traced curve during mental curve tracing. Houtkamp, R., Spekreijse, H., & Roelfsema, P. R. (2003). A gradual spread of attention during mental curve tracing. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 65(7), 1136–1144 — [DOI 10.3758/BF03194840](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194840) [V for citation]. Contested by a moving "zoom lens" account: Crundall, D., Dewhurst, R., & Underwood, G. (2008). *Perception & Psychophysics*, 70(2), 374–388 — "the results argue against a spreading trace of attention that encompasses the whole line" [V via search summary].
- Neural and computational account (monkey V1 plus model): Pooresmaeili & Roelfsema (2014) found that response enhancement spreads along the traced curve slowly when curves are nearby and faster when they are far apart. An "attentional growth-cone" model explains this: enhancement spreads through cortical areas with different receptive-field sizes at about 50 ms per receptive field, so the largest receptive field that fits between curves sets the speed. Pooresmaeili, A., & Roelfsema, P. R. (2014). A growth-cone model for the spread of object-based attention during contour grouping. *Current Biology*, 24(24), 2869–2877 — [record](https://publications.goettingen-research-online.de/ID-7942) [V for finding; P for volume/pages]
- Human extension to 2-D regions: grouping is fast for large, uniform areas and slower for narrow ones, and slower with greater distance between cued dots. Jeurissen, D., Self, M. W., & Roelfsema, P. R. (2016). Serial grouping of 2D-image regions with object-based attention in humans. *eLife*, 5, e14320 — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/14320) [V]
- **Crossings and crossing angle (node-link diagrams).** Huang (2008), eye tracking, 16 subjects, shortest-path search in graphs, with the path component fixed and crossings on the path varied:
  - Median path-task times: no crossings 6.81 s; near-90° crossings 14.74 s; small-angle crossings 29.41 s (all pairwise p ≤ .001).
  - With small-angle crossings there were "quite a lot of back-and-forth eye movements around the crossing points … the viewer was uncertain about which way to go." Near-90° crossings slowed eye movements but left them smooth.
  - Subject quote: "crossings affect me except at right angles."
  - Huang, W. (2008). An eye tracking study into the effects of graph layout. arXiv:0810.4431 — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0810.4431); also Huang, W., Hong, S.-H., & Eades, P. (2008). Effects of crossing angles. *Proc. IEEE PacificVis 2008*, 41–46 [V].
  - Status: a small preliminary study with few drawings; direction consistent with Ware et al. (below) and with path-counting follow-ups.
- Ware et al. (2002), cited by Huang: good path continuity (straightness through nodes) helps path search. For shortest-path tasks, "it is the number of edges that cross the shortest path itself that is important, rather than the total number of edge crossings." Ware, C., Purchase, H., Colpoys, L., & McGill, M. (2002). Cognitive measurements of graph aesthetics. *Information Visualization*, 1(2), 103–110 — quoted in [Huang 2008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0810.4431) [V for quotes; P for volume/pages]

### Inferences
- **Local scale should depend on clearance.** Tracing speed scales with the distance to the nearest other curve (zoom-lens/growth-cone). A fixed network that propagates labels along vessels, e.g. "same vessel" through a crossing, can therefore use a scale-adaptive step: step size ∝ local clearance (distance to the nearest non-target vessel). Near a junction, clearance → 0 and propagation should fall back to the finest scale. This also predicts where errors concentrate: near-coincident junctions and acute-angle crossings.
- **Acute crossings are inherently ambiguous.** Small-angle crossings are where human path-followers hesitate. For a vessel classifier, crossing angle is a confidence variable: confidence in X-vs-Y should rise with the angle between the two putative straight-through paths.
- **Use only crossings on the traced path.** Ware et al. suggest that what hurts is crossings *on* the traced path. Path-propagation networks should weight evidence from junctions along the path, not global clutter.

### Gaps
- **No direct study was found** of how human observers choose which branch "continues" when tracing through an X versus a Y. The rule (minimal turning angle, plus similarity of width/contrast) is extrapolated from good continuation, not measured in a curve-tracing paradigm.
- Absolute tracing speeds (e.g. ms per degree of visual angle) from Jolicoeur et al. (1986, 1991) and Houtkamp et al. (2003) could not be verified this session.
- The graph-crossing studies used schematic straight-line node-link diagrams with a small N, not natural branching images.

## 4. Occlusion and amodal completion: T-junctions, relatability, tangent discontinuities, border ownership

### Takeaway
T-junctions and other tangent discontinuities are the local trigger for occlusion-based completion. Without the proper junctions, completion is not perceived even when global relatability and surface similarity are present (Rubin 2001). Whether two edge fragments connect is well described by Kellman–Shipley relatability: their linear extensions must meet and the connecting curve must be smooth, monotonic and bend by no more than 90°. Within relatable pairs, strength is graded by support ratio and relative angle. This is one of the most directly computable rule sets in the literature.

### Cited Findings
- Rubin (2001), paradigm: displays matched in every occlusion cue except junction structure. L- and T-junctions were removed, or made locally inconsistent with the still-valid global occlusion interpretation. Observers reported no illusory contours or amodal completion in the junction-manipulated images, even though relatability and surface similarity were unchanged. The junction manipulations also changed the perceived stereoscopic depth and motion of image regions. Rubin's model: junctions, as a local occlusion cue, "launch" completion, and more global cues (relatability) act at a later stage. Rubin, N. (2001). The role of junctions in surface completion and contour matching. *Perception*, 30(3), 339–366 — [DOI 10.1068/p3173](https://doi.org/10.1068/p3173) [V]. Status: a single paper with several demonstrations. It is widely cited and consistent with Shipley & Kellman (1990).
- Kellman & Shipley (1991) defined relatability. Candidates for interpolation are edges ending in tangent discontinuities (junctions or corners), produced mainly by occlusion but also by L-junctions in illusory-contour displays. "A pair of input edges is relatable if they can be connected by a smooth, monotonic curve bending no more than 90 degrees." Interpolated edges match the orientation of the inducing edges at the discontinuity. Kellman, P. J., & Shipley, T. F. (1991). A theory of visual interpolation in object perception. *Cognitive Psychology*, 23(2), 141–221 — [DOI 10.1016/0010-0285(91)90009-D](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(91)90009-D); definition quoted in [Carrigan, Palmer & Kellman 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf) [V]
- Formal statement [P, not verified this session]: place edge E1 so it ends at the origin pointing along +x. Let E2 end at a point whose linear extension meets E1's extension. Let r and R be the lengths of the extensions of E1 and E2 to their intersection, and θ the angle of E2 relative to E1's extension. Then E1 and E2 are relatable iff 0 ≤ R·cos θ ≤ r.
- Singh & Hoffman (1999) proved that, under appropriate conditions, relatability is mathematically equivalent to the existence of a smooth interpolating curve *with no inflection points*. They proposed extending relatability to a graded rather than all-or-none measure. Singh, M., & Hoffman, D. D. (1999). Completing visual contours: The relationship between relatability and minimizing inflections. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 61, 943–951 — [abstract](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/images/personal-manish-singh/papers/singhhoffman99_abs.html); DOI [10.3758/BF03206908](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03206908) [V]
- Graded strength: within relatable edges, interpolation strength depends on **support ratio**, the ratio of physically specified edge length to total edge length (Shipley & Kellman 1992; Banton & Levi 1992), and on **relative angle** (Singh & Hoffman 1999) — [Carrigan, Palmer & Kellman 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf) [V]. Primary: Shipley, T. F., & Kellman, P. J. (1992). Strength of visual interpolation depends on the ratio of physically specified to total edge length. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 52(1), 97–106 [P].
- The 90° limit is contested. Boselie & Wouterlood (1992) argued against it with partly occluded triangle displays — [DOI 10.1007/BF01358265](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01358265). Carrigan et al. (2016) used an objective dot-localization paradigm and argued that such displays reflect global/recognition processes rather than true contour interpolation — [Carrigan et al. 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf) [V]. Precision and shape of interpolated contours: Fulvio, J. M., Singh, M., & Maloney, L. T. (2008). Precision and consistency of contour interpolation. *Vision Research*, 48(6), 831–849 (cited in Carrigan et al.) [V for citation].
- "Promiscuous interpolation": all relatable connections may form in an intermediate representation. Competition, border ownership, surface continuity or closure then decide whether they appear in the final scene description (Kellman, Guttman & Wickens 2001; Kellman, Garrigan & Shipley 2005). Relatability generalizes to 3-D contour and surface interpolation and to spatiotemporal interpolation — [Carrigan et al. 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf) [V]. Kellman, P. J., Garrigan, P., & Shipley, T. F. (2005). Object interpolation in three dimensions. *Psychological Review*, 112(3), 586–609 [P].
- Tangent discontinuities are necessary for illusory-contour formation. Shipley, T. F., & Kellman, P. J. (1990). The role of discontinuities in the perception of subjective figures. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 48(3), 259–270 — cited in [Carrigan et al. 2016](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2025/09/carrigan_palmer_kellman_2016.pdf) [V for citation]. Caveat: amodal completion can occur without image tangent discontinuities. Tse, P. U., & Albert, M. K. (1998). Amodal completion in the absence of image tangent discontinuities. *Perception*, 27, 455–464 [P].
- Real-image caveat: occlusion points in natural images are often not locally identifiable (McDermott 2004; Section 1) — [DOI 10.1068/p5265](https://doi.org/10.1068/p5265) [V]

### Inferences
- **Computable relatability test for vessel gaps** (e.g. a vessel passing under another vessel or a lesion, or a segmentation break). For two vessel ends with tangent directions t1, t2 and end points p1, p2:
  - (i) the forward rays from p1 along t1 and from p2 along t2 must intersect (or be collinear and aligned);
  - (ii) the total turn must be ≤ 90°;
  - (iii) the connecting curve must be inflection-free (Singh & Hoffman), equivalent to 0 ≤ R cos θ ≤ r;
  - (iv) connection strength is graded by support ratio (visible length / (visible + gap)) and by turning angle.
- **For a vessel crossing,** the occluded ("under") vessel's two arms should be mutually relatable with near-zero turn. In a bifurcation, the daughters are generally *not* relatable to each other across the junction, since they diverge. They are each partially relatable to the parent with turns typically below 90°.
- **T-junction cue in vessels.** Where one vessel's edges run uninterrupted through the junction and the other's edges terminate on them (T-junction at each edge), the uninterrupted vessel is the "occluder" or in-front candidate. In fundus images, overlap is closer to transparency than occlusion (Section 5), so this cue should be combined with the luminance test, not used alone.

### Gaps
- The exact algebraic form of relatability (R cos θ condition) was not verified from the 1991 primary text this session.
- Classical border-ownership psychophysics (e.g. figure-ground by convexity, extremal edges) was not covered in depth here. Most border-ownership evidence is neurophysiological (von der Heydt and colleagues), likely covered by other researchers.
- Relatability was developed for luminance edges of opaque surfaces, not for thin, semi-transparent tubular structures.

## 5. Transparency: X-junction luminance conditions, additive vs multiplicative models, mapping to absorbing media

### Takeaway
At an X-junction, the ordinal pattern of the four luminances decides whether transparency is seen:
- **Non-reversing** (contrast polarity preserved along both edges): transparency, with ambiguous/bistable depth order.
- **Single-reversing:** transparency with a unique depth order.
- **Double-reversing:** no transparency.

Metelli's episcotister (additive) model gives the classic polarity and magnitude constraints. Perceived transmittance, however, follows a ratio of Michelson contrasts (Singh & Anderson 2002). Anderson's rule says the lower-contrast side of a polarity-preserving junction is split into two layers. For absorbing media such as overlapping blood columns, intensities multiply (Beer–Lambert), so optical densities add. This yields an X-junction consistency test in log-intensity space that is straightforward to compute.

### Cited Findings
- Metelli's episcotister model: a rotating disk with an open sector transmits fraction α of the background and contributes its own reflectance t for the remaining (1 − α). In luminance terms (Gerbino et al. 1990): P = αA + (1 − α)T and Q = αB + (1 − α)T, so **α = (P − Q)/(A − B)** and **T = (AQ − BP)/(A + Q − B − P)**. Here A, B are the plain-view background luminances and P, Q the same regions seen through the layer. Restricting 0 ≤ α ≤ 1 gives the **polarity constraint** (P–Q must have the same sign as A–B) and the **magnitude constraint** (|P − Q| ≤ |A − B|). Figural conditions: the underlying contour must continue without jumps where it meets the layer boundary, the two halves of the layer must form one coherent surface, and the layer boundary must be continuous at the crossing — [Singh & Anderson 2002, PDF](https://ruccs.sas.rutgers.edu/images/archive/personal-manish-singh/papers/singh_anderson_2002a.pdf) [V]. Primary: Metelli, F. (1974). The perception of transparency. *Scientific American*, 230(4), 90–98 — [DOI 10.1038/scientificamerican0474-90](https://doi.org/10.1038/scientificamerican0474-90) [P]
- Adelson & Anandan (1990): ordinal relations among the four luminances at an X-junction classify it as non-, single- or double-reversing. Non- and single-reversing junctions support transparency; single-reversing junctions give an unambiguous depth order, non-reversing junctions an ambiguous one; double-reversing junctions do not support transparency. Adelson, E. H., & Anandan, P. (1990). Ordinal characteristics of transparency. *AAAI-90 Workshop on Qualitative Vision*, Boston, pp. 77–81 — [abstract](https://persci.mit.edu/pub_abstracts/ordinal90_abs.html) [V]. Equivalent classification (Z-, C- and criss-cross configurations) in Beck & Ivry (1988), shown in Fig. 13 of [Singh & Anderson 2002](https://ruccs.sas.rutgers.edu/images/archive/personal-manish-singh/papers/singh_anderson_2002a.pdf) [V]. Status: robust and widely replicated as a qualitative rule.
- Anderson's (1997) qualitative scission rule: "When two aligned contours undergo a discontinuous change in contrast magnitude, but preserve contrast polarity, the lower contrast region is decomposed into two causal layers" (p. 420). Anderson's (1999) anchoring principle: the highest-contrast regions are treated as unobscured (α = 1), and lower-contrast regions as seen through a transparent layer. Anderson, B. L. (1997). A theory of illusory lightness and transparency in monocular and binocular images: the role of contour junctions. *Perception*, 26(4), 419–453 — [DOI 10.1068/p260419](https://doi.org/10.1068/p260419); rule quoted in [Singh & Anderson 2002](https://ruccs.sas.rutgers.edu/images/archive/personal-manish-singh/papers/singh_anderson_2002a.pdf) [V]
- **Ratio-of-contrasts model.** Paradigm: transmittance matching with stereoscopic disks over sinusoidal or variegated backgrounds (Michelson contrasts about 0.118–0.47). Matches were linear in luminance range but became essentially independent of mean luminance once expressed in Michelson contrast, C = (Lmax − Lmin)/(Lmax + Lmin). Perceived transmittance ≈ **C_inside / C_surround**: Michelson contrast in the transparent region divided by Michelson contrast in plain view. Michelson contrast is also "a critical image variable to initiate percepts of transparency." This explains Metelli's puzzle that a dark episcotister looks more transmissive than a light one of equal physical α. Observers overestimate the transmittance of darkening layers and underestimate that of lightening layers. At X-junctions, luminance difference and Michelson contrast can disagree about which side is "lower contrast", and the paper analyses that case. Singh, M., & Anderson, B. L. (2002). Toward a perceptual theory of transparency. *Psychological Review*, 109(3), 492–519 — [DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.109.3.492](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.109.3.492); [PDF](https://ruccs.sas.rutgers.edu/images/archive/personal-manish-singh/papers/singh_anderson_2002a.pdf) [V]
- Kasrai & Kingdom (2001) found that both the luminance-based Metelli model and a ratio-of-Michelson-contrasts model predicted transmittance settings best among those tested. Kasrai, R., & Kingdom, F. A. A. (2001). Precision, accuracy, and range of perceived achromatic transparency. *JOSA A*, 18(1), 1–11 — [PDF](https://www.mvr.mcgill.ca/Fred/Papers/Kasrai&Kingdom_2001.pdf) [V via search summary]. Robilotto, Khang & Zaidi (2002, *Journal of Vision*, 2(5):3) likewise favour contrast-based accounts [P].
- Filter (absorbing-layer) models: Beck, Prazdny & Ivry (1984) proposed a filter model with repeated reflections between layer and background, an alternative to the episcotister. Beck, J., Prazdny, K., & Ivry, R. (1984). The perception of transparency with achromatic colors. *Perception & Psychophysics*, 35(5), 407–422 — [DOI 10.3758/BF03203917](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03203917); described in [Wikipedia: Perceptual transparency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptual_transparency) [V for the description]. Faul & Ekroll developed a subtractive/multiplicative filter model for chromatic transparency. Faul, F., & Ekroll, V. (2002). Psychophysical model of chromatic perceptual transparency based on subtractive color mixture. *JOSA A*, 19(6), 1084–1095 — [DOI 10.1364/JOSAA.19.001084](https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.19.001084); Faul, F., & Ekroll, V. (2011). On the filter approach to perceptual transparency. *Journal of Vision*, 11(7):7 — [DOI 10.1167/11.7.7](https://doi.org/10.1167/11.7.7) [P]
- In line drawings (no luminance), X-junctions where outlines cross can also signal transparency. Sayim, B., & Cavanagh, P. (2011). The art of transparency. *i-Perception*, 2(7), 679–696 (DOI not verified) [P]

### Inferences
- **Absorbing media map onto multiplicative transparency.** For thin absorbing layers with no scattering or emission (a reasonable first approximation for a blood column against a reflecting fundus or a backlit field), Beer–Lambert gives I = I0·10^(−OD). Two overlapping vessels give I_AB = I0·T_A·T_B, i.e. OD_AB = OD_A + OD_B. Writing L = log I, a true crossing satisfies the **log-additivity identity** L_AB − L_A − L_B + L_0 ≈ 0. Here L_0 is background, L_A and L_B are each vessel alone near the junction, and L_AB is the overlap. A bifurcation has no doubly attenuated region: the junction core has L ≈ L_parent, not L_A + L_B − L_0.
  - This gives a direct fixed-weight computation: a 4-sector linear filter in log space whose output is ≈ 0 for crossings and ≈ −OD_B (strongly non-zero) for a "fake overlap".
  - It is a physics-based inference, not a human psychophysics result. It ignores specular light reflexes, multiple scattering, partial-volume blur and vessel-diameter-dependent path length.
- **Expected ordinal type.** In this model, overlapping dark vessels on a bright background produce **non-reversing** X-junctions: background > A > AB and B > AB. Adelson–Anandan predicts bistable depth order for these. Photometry alone therefore will not say which vessel is on top. The literature implies depth order must come from other cues, e.g. continuity of the light reflex or relatability of edges. Retinal datasets do label artery-over-vein vs vein-over-artery (RETA; Section 6).
- **Contrast-based transmittance estimate.** Singh & Anderson suggest estimating each vessel's perceived transmittance as the ratio of Michelson contrasts across a background edge seen through vs. outside the vessel. Under the multiplicative model this ratio is constant along the vessel only if the vessel is a pure filter, which offers a consistency check along each arm.
- **Polarity check.** A double-reversing pattern at a putative X, e.g. the overlap brighter than either arm, indicates the structure is not two overlapping absorbers. It might be a light reflex, an artefact, or a non-vascular crossing.

### Gaps
- No psychophysical study was found that tests human transparency perception specifically for **thin tubular absorbing structures** (vessels or neurites) or for log-additive (multiplicative) X-junctions. Mapping the transparency literature onto vessels is therefore an inference.
- A 2007 dissent (Masin & Da Pos) reportedly favouring Metelli's model over the ratio-of-contrasts model surfaced in search snippets but could not be verified or cited properly this session.
- The quantitative form of Faul & Ekroll's filter model was not re-checked this session.

## 6. Branching vs crossing perception specifically: cross/touch (stream/bounce), tree/vascular structure, and expert annotator behaviour on retinal crossings vs bifurcations

### Takeaway
Direct psychophysics on static "cross vs touch/branch" judgments is sparse. The closest paradigm is dynamic stream/bounce: the default human percept for ambiguous coincidence is *streaming* (continuation through the crossing). Transients at the coincidence point push toward bouncing, and X-junction depth cues promote streaming. In retinal imaging, junction ground truth comes mostly from single annotators or automated skeleton rules, and junction counts differ about 2× between datasets. Published inter-observer agreement specifically for crossing vs bifurcation labels was not found. Even curated datasets acknowledge that crossovers are where vessel edges are "always hard to determine" and that near-coincident bifurcations merge.

### Cited Findings
- Stream/bounce (dynamic): two identical discs move toward each other, overlap and separate. Without other cues, observers predominantly see streaming (passing through). A brief sound near the moment of coincidence promotes "bounce". Sekuler, R., Sekuler, A. B., & Lau, R. (1997). Sound alters visual motion perception. *Nature*, 385, 308 — [DOI 10.1038/385308a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/385308a0) [P]. Visual flashes at coincidence, pauses in motion and attentional distractors also increase bounce reports. The streaming default has been attributed to "inertial" motion integration, which favours a straight path over a returning path — [summary of stream/bounce literature, AP&P 2011 (Kyoto repository)](https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dspace/bitstream/2433/143581/1/s13414-011-0126-6.pdf) [V via search summary]. Status: streaming default and sound-induced bouncing are robust and widely replicated.
- Cross-species: chimpanzees showed no default streaming bias, but depth cues including X-junctions and local motion coherence promoted streaming — [Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 2011, DOI 10.3758/s13414-011-0126-6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-011-0126-6) [V via search summary; authors not verified]
- Graph-reading studies with crossing lines (Section 3): small-angle crossings cause hesitation about "which way to go" — [Huang 2008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0810.4431) [V]
- **Retinal junction annotation practice.**
  - The RETA benchmark (81 vessel masks) derived bifurcations automatically from the skeleton: skeleton pixels with ≥3 neighbours are bifurcation vertices, and the annotator validated only abnormal ones with >3 edges.
  - Crossovers were annotated manually during artery/vein separation. The annotator zoomed into each crossover, "typically connecting 4 to 6 segments", identified venous segments, re-linked disconnected arterial segments by manual image completion, and labelled each crossover artery-over-vein or vein-over-artery.
  - A single trained annotator did the correction work "so as to prevent inter-annotator bias", and **no inter- or intra-annotator agreement statistics were reported for junctions.**
  - The authors "paid special attention to vessel boundaries and crossovers where vessel edges are always hard to determine," and note that when two bifurcations are very close, graph construction "inevitably merges them as 1 vertex."
  - Lyu, X., Cheng, L., & Zhang, S. (2022). The RETA benchmark for retinal vascular tree analysis. *Scientific Data*, 9, 397 — [DOI 10.1038/s41597-022-01507-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01507-y); [PMC9273761](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9273761/) [V]
- Inconsistent junction ground truth across datasets: IOSTAR has roughly half as many ground-truth junctions per image as DRIVE, so a junction-detection study trained only on DRIVE — [arXiv:1806.03175, "A Multi-task Network to Detect Junctions in Retinal Vasculature" (2018)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.03175) [V via search summary]
- Automated junction detection and classification reaches F1 of about 70% on DRIVE and about 60% on IOSTAR. Zhao, H., et al. (2020). Retinal vascular junction detection and classification via deep neural networks. *Computer Methods and Programs in Biomedicine* — [PDF](https://pcwww.liv.ac.uk/~hezhao/papers-pp/2020before/[CMBP20]%20Retinal%20vascular%20junction%20detection%20and%20classification%20via%20deep%20neural%20networks.pdf) [V via search summary]. A rule-based skeleton/geometry method reported crossover recall of 87.6% and precision of 90.5% on DRIVE/STARE (Hamad et al. 2014) — [Image Analysis & Stereology](https://ias-iss.org/ojs/IAS/article/view/1101) [V via search summary]. These scores are measured against single-reference ground truth.
- Pixel-level vessel segmentation agreement between two human observers is only moderate (mean Dice reported as 77.65% in a recent subject-separated evaluation). This is not junction-specific — [arXiv 2609.25597](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25597) [V via search summary; dataset not confirmed]

### Inferences
- The streaming default suggests a perceptual prior favouring **continuation (crossing) over contact (bounce/branch)** when the evidence is ambiguous and nothing marks the junction. For vessels, a fixed network could adopt a prior that favours the "crossing" interpretation when two straight-through pairings exist with small turning angles. Evidence for a bifurcation would then be a sharp local event at the junction: width change consistent with Murray's-law-like splitting, absence of a doubly attenuated core (Section 5), or arms that do not pair collinearly. The link from stream/bounce to static vessels is speculative.
- Retinal ground truth is generated with single annotators and skeleton heuristics, and close bifurcations merge. Benchmark labels at near-coincident junctions (e.g. a bifurcation within a few vessel widths of a crossing) are therefore likely the noisiest. A fixed-weight classifier's "errors" there may partly reflect label ambiguity.

### Gaps
- **No published inter-observer agreement (kappa or similar) specifically for crossing vs bifurcation labels in retinal images was found** this session (searched DRIVE, IOSTAR, VARIA, VICAVR, RETA). The original junction-annotation papers were not retrieved and should be checked: Azzopardi & Petkov 2013 *Pattern Recognition Letters* for DRIVE junction ground truth; Abbasi-Sureshjani et al. 2016 ISBI for IOSTAR junctions.
- No study found on a **static** stream/bounce analogue (judging whether two drawn lines cross or touch/kiss) or on human perception of tree or vascular branching structure as such.
- Neuron-reconstruction inter-annotator variability at branch points and crossovers (e.g. DIADEM/BigNeuron) was not searched this session.

## 7. Crowding and resolution limits near junctions: when do two junctions or contours merge perceptually?

### Takeaway
In the periphery, flankers within a critical spacing roughly proportional to eccentricity are integrated with the target (Bouma's law). A recent 50-observer study gives a Bouma factor of about 0.23 and a foveal crowding distance of about 0.08° (about 4.8 arcmin), with tangential crowding about half of radial. Contour integration and crowding may share a mechanism: an integration field whose minimum size scales with eccentricity. Curve-tracing and grouping slow sharply when curves come close. Separation relative to local scale is the governing variable, which maps onto vessel-width-relative separation of near-coincident junctions.

### Cited Findings
- Bouma's rule: flankers within about half the target eccentricity crowd the target, and Bouma later reduced the constant from 0.5 to 0.4. Critical spacing is proportional to eccentricity and largely independent of target size — [Coates et al. 2021, PMC8556556](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8556556/) [V]; [Wikipedia: Visual crowding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_crowding) [V]. Primary: Bouma, H. (1970). Interaction effects in parafoveal letter recognition. *Nature*, 226, 177–178 — [DOI 10.1038/226177a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/226177a0) [P]; Pelli, D. G., & Tillman, K. A. (2008). The uncrowded window of object recognition. *Nature Neuroscience*, 11(10), 1129–1135 — [DOI 10.1038/nn.2187](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2187) [P]. Status: very robust.
- Kurzawski et al. (2023), paradigm: 13 crowding distances measured in 50 observers at 0°, 5° and 10° eccentricity. A two-parameter linear Bouma law explained 82% of variance (cross-validated). Bouma factor ≈ 0.23 (standardized 0.24). Foveal crowding distance ≈ 0.08° (≈ 4.8 arcmin), close to foveal acuity of about 0.07°. Tangential:radial crowding-distance ratio = 0.55. Kurzawski, J. W., Burchell, A., Thapa, D., Winawer, J., Majaj, N. J., & Pelli, D. G. (2023). The Bouma law accounts for crowding in 50 observers. *Journal of Vision*, 23(8), 6 — [DOI 10.1167/jov.23.8.6](https://doi.org/10.1167/jov.23.8.6); [PMC10408772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10408772/) [V]
- In dense displays, only the target's nearest neighbours affected performance, which challenges a fixed crowding window. Bornet et al. (2021). Shrinking Bouma's window: How to model crowding in dense displays — [PMC8284675](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8284675/) [V via search summary]
- Crowding and contour integration may share mechanisms. Pelli, Palomares & Majaj (2004) proposed integration fields whose minimum size scales with eccentricity, so crowding occurs when the field is forced to be too large for the task. May & Hess (2007) explained the faster peripheral decline of ladder-contour detection with a crowding-type model — [Hess, May & Dumoulin 2014](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18317/1/Hess_May_Dumoulin_2013.pdf) [V]
- Proximity of other curves slows tracing and grouping: tracing rate depends on the distance to nearby distractor curves ([Springer 10.3758/BF03198493](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03198493)). Attention spreads slowly when curves are nearby ([Pooresmaeili & Roelfsema 2014](https://publications.goettingen-research-online.de/ID-7942)). Grouping is slower for narrow regions ([Jeurissen et al. 2016](https://elifesciences.org/articles/14320)) [V]
- Ground-truth construction also merges very close bifurcations into one graph vertex ([RETA, Lyu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9273761/)) [V]
- Real-image junction recognition improves with aperture size up to about 100 px ([McDermott 2004](https://doi.org/10.1068/p5265)) [V]. The context needed to interpret a junction is several times larger than the junction itself.

### Inferences
- **Scale-relative separation.** By analogy with the Bouma ratio, a fixed-weight network should treat two junctions closer than a critical separation, expressed relative to local vessel width, as one compound junction. Example: a bifurcation at distance d from a crossing, with d/width below a threshold. It should then classify the compound configuration (e.g. "crossing + nearby bifurcation", or a 4-arm vs 5-arm node) rather than forcing two independent labels. The psychophysical constant (≈0.23–0.5 × eccentricity) is defined for retinal eccentricity, not image scale. Only the *principle* transfers: interference zones scale with the integration field, and the field scales with local scale. The vessel threshold must be set empirically.
- **Anisotropy.** Radial (along-axis) crowding zones are about 2× tangential. By analogy, interference between junctions may be stronger along the vessel axis than across it, which argues for elongated (anisotropic) junction-merging neighbourhoods aligned with vessel direction. This is speculative.
- **Clearance-dependent processing.** Tracing slows in proportion to curve proximity. Any propagation or label-passing scheme should shrink its spatial step near closely spaced structures; it can use large steps along isolated vessels.

### Gaps
- No psychophysical study was found that directly measures the minimum separation at which two *junctions* (as opposed to letters, Gabors or curves) are perceived as distinct, or how a bifurcation adjacent to a crossing is perceived.
- Two-line resolution and contour-interaction literature (e.g. Flom, Weymouth & Kahneman 1963) was not retrieved this session. Foveal two-line separation thresholds should be checked if needed.
- Crowding data concern peripheral viewing. Expert annotators inspect junctions foveally and with zoom, so their limits are set by image resolution and blur rather than by crowding.
